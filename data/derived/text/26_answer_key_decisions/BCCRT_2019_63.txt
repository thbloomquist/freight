INTRODUCTION

1. This dispute is about unpaid freight delivery charges. The applicant carrier, ACME
    TRANSPORT LTD, claims payment of $4,450 for 2 invoices, plus interest. The
    respondent freight broker, PLANET EXPRESS TRANSPORT LTD, says the third
    party shipper, RBI, reported 1 of the 2 loads was damaged, and alleges the
    applicant failed to process an insurance claim. The respondent submits that
    because it was not paid by RBI, it does not have to pay the applicant.

2. The applicant is represented by Amarjit Kailey, an employee or principal. The
    respondent is represented by Jaskam Ke, an employee or principal.

3. For the reasons that follow, I allow the applicant’s claims.


JURISDICTION AND PROCEDURE

4. These are the formal written reasons of the Civil Resolution Tribunal (tribunal). The
    tribunal has jurisdiction over small claims brought under section 118 of the Civil
    Resolution Tribunal Act (Act). The tribunal’s mandate is to provide dispute
    resolution services accessibly, quickly, economically, informally, and flexibly. In
    resolving disputes, the tribunal must apply principles of law and fairness, and
    recognize any relationships between parties to a dispute that will likely continue
    after the dispute resolution process has ended.

5. The tribunal has discretion to decide the format of the hearing, including by writing,
    telephone, videoconferencing, email, or a combination of these. In the
    circumstances here, I find that I am properly able to assess and weigh the
    documentary evidence and submissions before me. Further, bearing in mind the
    tribunal’s mandate that includes proportionality and a speedy resolution of disputes,
    I find that an oral hearing is not necessary. I also note that in Yas v. Pope, 2018
    BCSC 282 at paragraphs 32 to 38, the BC Supreme Court recognized the tribunal’s
    process and found that oral hearings are not necessarily required where credibility
    is in issue. The tribunal may accept as evidence information that it considers

    relevant, necessary and appropriate, whether or not the information would be
    admissible in a court of law. The tribunal may also ask questions of the parties and
    witnesses and inform itself in any other way it considers appropriate.

6. Under tribunal rule 126, in resolving this dispute the tribunal may order a party to do
    or stop doing something, order a party to pay money, or order any other terms or
    conditions the tribunal considers appropriate.

7. To the extent the parties respectively make allegations of defamation, I have not
    considered them, because defamation is expressly outside the tribunal’s jurisdiction.


ISSUE

8. The issue in this dispute is to what extent, if any, the applicant is entitled to payment
    of its invoices totaling $4,450, plus interest, for 2 freight deliveries.


EVIDENCE AND ANALYSIS

9. In a civil claim such as this, the applicant bears the burden of proof on a balance of
    probabilities. I have only addressed the evidence and submissions below as
    necessary to explain my decision.

10. It is undisputed the applicant hauled 2 loads at the respondent’s request: #5101 and
    #5098. I find the respondent is responsible to pay the applicant’s invoices, as
    discussed below.

11. Invoice #5101, dated August 17, 2016, is for $2,220 for an August 13, 2016 delivery
    from a combination of various Lower Mainland shippers to Edmonton. This
    shipment contained kitchen cabinets, mouldings, and related construction items.
    There is no suggestion that invoice #5101 was delivered damaged or that there was
    any other concern about this shipment. There is no explanation before me as to why
    the respondent has not paid the applicant’s invoice #5101, and there is no evidence
    the applicant’s calculations are incorrect. I order the respondent to pay invoice
    #5101.

                                               3
12. Invoice #5098, dated November 26, 2016, is for $2,250, for an August 15, 2016
   delivery from AVS Windows in Surrey, BC to RBI Construction (RBI) in Calgary, AB.
   It appears RBI was the entity who hired the respondent, and as noted above, the
   respondent hired the applicant to carry and deliver the goods.

13. Invoice #5098 includes $450 in “waiting time”. The bill of lading shows the driver
   arrived at 9 a.m. and left at 8:15 p.m. This supports the applicant’s undisputed
   submission that the receiver at RBI spent a significant amount of time inspecting the
   load. As set out on the bill of lading, this shipment contained 74 pieces, with the
   item description “windows”. On its face, the bill of lading required written notice to
   the applicant carrier of any damage within 60 days of delivery. As submitted by the
   applicant, there is no indication on the bill of lading of any concern about the
   delivery, and the receiver RBI signed for it.

14. Based on the evidence before me, on December 19, 2016 RBI’s lawyer first wrote
   to the applicant about alleged damage to “more than 75%” of the windows, well
   after the 60-day limit set out in the bill of lading for shipment #5098. While the
   respondent stated in a similar January 24, 2017 letter that it immediately contacted
   the applicant about the damaged windows, there is no evidence that it did so and no
   evidence that it did so in writing as required. RBI is not a party to this dispute, which
   is about the applicant’s demand for payment of its invoices issued to the
   respondent.

15. The respondent asked the applicant to pursue an insurance claim for shipment
   #5098, which is mentioned in their January 24, 2017 letter. The applicant did so by
   early February 2017. It is unclear what came of this claim, but I find it does not
   impact the respondent’s obligation to pay the applicant. I say this because I find the
   evidence before me shows the windows were delivered undamaged, and even if
   they were damaged, the applicant is not responsible for the damage.

16. In particular, as referenced above it is undisputed both shipments were delivered
   with clean bills of lading and receiver signatures. RBI’s receiver inspected the
   shipment thoroughly while the applicant’s driver waited all day, for almost 12 hours.

                                            4
    I find there was no defect or damage found on the receiver’s detailed inspection of
    the shipment. If 75% of the windows had been damaged at the time of delivery, I
    find it more likely that RBI’s receiver would have noticed it during its detailed
    inspection.

17. Further, as set out in the bill of lading, if there is any damage, the carrier must be
    notified within 60 days of delivery. Based on the evidence before me, no one
    notified the applicant in writing within 60 days. Based on the documentation before
    me, the first time the respondent raised a concern was February 1, 2017, more than
    6 months after delivery, and RBI’s letter was in December 2016, outside the 60 day
    timeframe. I find the bill of lading governed the parties’ conduct about damage
    claims.

18. I acknowledge the respondent provided photos of damaged windows. However, as
    noted by the applicant, these photos are undated and on the face of them I cannot
    tell which shipment they come from or when the damage occurred. The respondent
    does not deny it brokered another shipment of windows for RBI which another
    carrier delivered. Even if these photos are of shipment #5098, I cannot tell if the
    windows were damaged after the applicant delivered them. I prefer the receiver’s
    clean bill of lading as the better evidence that the windows were delivered by the
    applicant undamaged. As also noted by the applicant, the respondent’s
    correspondence refers to a damaged shipment containing 113 pieces, but shipment
    #5098 only had 74 pieces. The respondent says the claim shows 113 pieces
    “because some pieces count is not the windows”. This explanation is unclear and
    ultimately not helpful, given the bill of lading clearly identified only 74 pieces being
    shipped, total. This discrepancy leads me to conclude the respondent’s evidence is
    not reliable.

19. I do acknowledge an email thread between the parties and an insurer. There is one
    entry that indicates the applicant’s driver discovered “window was broken in
    Calgary” and the RBI drop off site was a construction zone (all quotes reproduced
    as written). It is unclear what was intended by that comment, but I find it does not


                                             5
    suggest negligence by the applicant. The statement went on to say “No accident
    report to the company from the driver maybe strapped too tight owner thinks it is
    common in that loss location”. The evidence before me does not indicate the
    applicant carrier was responsible for loading and strapping the windows in shipment
    #5098. Thus, even if the windows were damaged during transport, the evidence
    does not show it was due to the applicant’s negligence.

20. I note the respondent provided a voice recording, which is legal in Canada if one
    party to the recording is aware the recording is being made. However, there is no
    indication of who the speakers are, whether either of them knew they were being
    recorded, and the speakers are not speaking in English. The respondent did not
    provide any English translation and made no submissions about the recording.
    There is nothing I can draw from this non-English recording and I place no weight
    on it.

21. Contrary to the respondent’s central submission, the fact that the respondent has
    not received payment from RBI for the applicant’s services is not relevant to the
    respondent’s obligation to pay the applicant. I have found above the applicant
    delivered #5098 undamaged and as also noted there was no issue with shipment
    #5101. I find the respondent must pay the applicant their invoices.

22. The respondent’s point in this dispute is that it has not been paid by RBI, because
    RBI was not paid through the applicant’s insurance. There is no evidence before me
    that requires the applicant to pursue an insurance claim for RBI, in order to receive
    payment from the respondent. As noted above, there is no dispute the applicant
    delivered the 2 shipments, as the respondent hired it to do. There is no dispute
    about the amount of the delivery charges. I have found the applicant delivered the
    goods undamaged and in any event there is no evidence before me that the
    applicant was negligent. Nothing turns on the insurance issue.

23. I also note that the respondent submits, “If I can get the money from RBI, we can
    pay you in full no problem at all”. I note the respondent’s submission it started a
    separate tribunal proceeding against RBI and the respondent’s reference to a

                                            6
    provincial court action, which I infer relates to an enforcement proceeding. Again,
    payment by RBI is an issue between the respondent and RBI, which is not a party
    to the dispute before me. I reject the respondent’s submission that shipment #5098
    is pending and that its shipment is not concluded. The load was delivered under the
    parties’ agreement, as noted by the receiver’s signature.

24. I turn to the applicant’s interest claim of $285.55. I note the applicant’s invoice terms
    are “net 30”, and on their face state that 2% monthly interest (24% per year) applies
    to all overdue accounts.

25. The 2% monthly interest rate equals 24% annual interest, which I find would result
    in a total interest calculation of $2,207.32 for both invoices, to the date of this
    decision. However, as noted the applicant claimed only the specific amount of
    $285.55 in interest for both invoices, and so that is what I allow.

26. In any event, I note there is an argument to be made that contractual interest (unlike
    pre-judgment interest under the Court Order Interest Act) is a substantive claim that
    must together with the principal debt fall under the tribunal’s $5,000 monetary limit
    (see Telus Services Inc. v. Hussey, 2016 BCPC 41 and Canadian Tire Bank v.
    Konkin, 2018 BCPC 151, although these cases considered the Small Claims Act,
    rather than the Act). I mention this here given the significant difference between the
    $285.55 claimed and the $2,207.32 calculation based on 24% annual interest. In
    other words, given the tribunal’s $5,000 monetary limit it is possible the applicant’s
    total contractual interest in this tribunal proceeding would be limited to $550, from
    the invoices’ due dates to the date of this decision. However, I do not need to
    decide that issue here, as the applicant limited their interest claim to $285.55.

27. In accordance with the Act and the tribunal rules, ordinarily a successful applicant is
    entitled to reimbursement of tribunal fees and reasonable dispute-related expenses.
    The applicant was successful. I therefore find it is entitled to reimbursement of $175
    in tribunal fees and $31.50 for a company search.

ORDERS

28. Within 14 days of this decision, I order the respondent to pay the applicant a total of
    $4,942.05, broken down as follows:

       a. $4,450 in debt for outstanding invoices for shipments #5101 and #5098,

       b. $285.55 in pre-judgment interest, and

       c. $206.50, for $175 in tribunal fees and $31.50 in dispute-related expenses.

29. The applicant is entitled to post-judgment interest, as applicable.

30. Under section 48 of the Act, the tribunal will not provide the parties with the Order
    giving final effect to this decision until the time for making a notice of objection
    under section 56.1(2) has expired and no notice of objection has been made. The
    time for filing a notice of objection is 28 days after the party receives notice of the
    tribunal’s final decision.

31. Under section 58.1 of the Act, a validated copy of the tribunal’s order can be
    enforced through the Provincial Court of British Columbia. A tribunal order can only
    be enforced if it is an approved consent resolution order, or, if no objection has
    been made and the time for filing a notice of objection has passed. Once filed, a
    tribunal order has the same force and effect as an order of the Provincial Court of
    British Columbia.
