INTRODUCTION

1.    This dispute is about alleged non-payment for services completed by the applicant,
      C&D Logistics Ltd., for the respondent, Salmon’s Rentals Ltd. In particular, the

                                           1
     applicant claims a total of $4,208.90 for payment of 2 invoices, inclusive of
     interest.

2.   The respondent simply says that it reasonably refuses to pay because it had to
     refund its client “due to the lack of execution” by the applicant as originally agreed.
     The applicant is represented by David Miao, an employee or officer. The
     respondent is represented by Shane Bozzer, who appears to be an employee or
     officer.


JURISDICTION AND PROCEDURE

3.   These are the formal written reasons of the Civil Resolution Tribunal (tribunal). The
     tribunal has jurisdiction over small claims brought under section 3.1 of the Civil
     Resolution Tribunal Act (Act). The tribunal’s mandate is to provide dispute
     resolution services accessibly, quickly, economically, informally, and flexibly. In
     resolving disputes, the tribunal must apply principles of law and fairness, and
     recognize any relationships between parties to a dispute that will likely continue
     after the dispute resolution process has ended.

4.   The tribunal has discretion to decide the format of the hearing, including by writing,
     telephone, videoconferencing, email, or a combination of these. I decided to hear
     this dispute through written submissions, because I find I can fairly decide the
     dispute based on the documentary evidence and submissions before me, without
     holding an oral hearing.

5.   The tribunal may accept as evidence information that it considers relevant,
     necessary and appropriate, whether or not the information would be admissible in
     a court of law. The tribunal may also ask questions of the parties and witnesses
     and inform itself in any other way it considers appropriate.

6.   Under tribunal rule 126, in resolving this dispute the tribunal may: order a party to
     do or stop doing something, order a party to pay money, or order any other terms
     or conditions the tribunal considers appropriate.


                                             2
ISSUES

7.   The issues in this dispute are to what extent, if any, the respondent owes the
     applicant $4,208.90 for unpaid invoices and associated interest.


EVIDENCE AND ANALYSIS

8.   I have only commented on the evidence and submissions to the extent necessary
     to give context to these reasons. In a civil dispute such as this, the applicant bears
     the burden of proof on a balance of probabilities.

9.   The applicant provided transportation services for 2 shipments of chairs and
     tables, which the respondent’s customer had rented:
      a. Shipment #1: To be picked up July 6, 2017 from Edmonton, and delivered to
          the Vancouver Convention Centre (VCC) for Friday July 7 “1pm-3pm
          window”.

      b. Shipment #2: To be picked up July 9, 2017 from the VCC at 1:00 a.m., and
          return it back to Edmonton. The second shipment was delivered to Edmonton
          on July 13, 2017.

10. As referenced above, the applicant claims payment of $4,208.90 for 2 invoices:
      a. Invoice #1033012 dated July 6, 2017: $1,312.50, plus $148.92 in interest at
          26.8% per annum (Shipment #1), and

      b. Invoice #1033013 dated July 10, 2017: $2,467.50, plus $279.98 in interest
          (Shipment #2). This reflects a $600 “after hours pick up” charge, and a $200
          “discount for late pick up”, because the pick-up did not happen until 2 p.m.

11. The respondent’s issue with the applicant’s services is two-fold. First, the
     respondent says the chairs were a “tangled mess” upon delivery to VCC, as they
     had not been lashed down during transport. The respondent’s rental agreement
     indicates the chairs were to be loaded on the truck in stacks of 8, with the tables
     on pallets. The text messages in evidence indicate the applicant acknowledged the


                                            3
     chairs had been loaded improperly. The respondent also says the rented items
     were “filthy” and several chairs and 2 tables were damaged due to poor packing.
     On the bill of lading for shipment #1, it was delivered at 2 p.m. Significantly, the
     fact that the respondent was unhappy with the way the container was loaded was
     noted on the bill of lading.

12. The applicant says the Shipment #1 was delivered to VCC in a timely manner and
     it received no reports of any concerns. I do not agree, because of the notation on
     the bill of lading. The respondent says the improperly loading of the chairs led to
     increased time and labour charges in unloading them, which I accept given the
     photos in evidence.

13. The applicant acknowledges that Shipment #2 was not picked up at 1:00 a.m., as
     expected, due to some confusion with the applicant’s carrier. The applicant says
     that when it was notified, it immediately resolved the problem by sending in the
     carrier as quickly as possible, and sent additional labour at its own cost to support
     the loading process. Based on the evidence before me, the applicant’s carrier
     picked up Shipment #2 around 2 p.m. on July 9, 2017. The applicant says in good
     faith it discounted Shipment #2 by $200, which is reflected in the claim in this
     dispute. While this is true, I note the applicant’s invoice still leaves a net $400
     charge for an “after hours” pick up that did not happen.

14. Because the applicant did not pick up the shipment at 1:00 a.m. on July 9, 2017 as
     expected, the respondent’s customer had to move the goods from the loading bay
     to a temporary storage area. The customer texted the respondent that “this was a
     total mess from start to finish … my staff ended up moving the stuff twice!”. The
     respondent advised the applicant on November 9, 2017 that in addition to the
     “state of the rentals” issue, it incurred $3,250 in added charges: $1,300 in extra
     moving hours on July 7, 2017 and $950 in extra moving hours on July 9, 2017,
     plus $1,000 for storage of the goods after the applicant failed to pick up Shipment
     #2 on time. The respondent’s November 9, 2017 email to the applicant was that
     “my client” was forced to incur significant labor charges, and the respondent’s


                                            4
     client is not a party to this dispute. The respondent submits that it had to refund its
     client.

15. On July 19, 2017, Mr. Bozzer emailed the applicant about the damage on
     shipment #1 that went to VCC, 12 days after the original delivery. Within 15
     minutes of Mr. Bozzer’s advice of damage, the applicant sent him paperwork to
     complete in order to file a cargo claim. I agree with the respondent that there was
     no need for the respondent to make a cargo claim, because the respondent was
     not claiming compensation for damaged goods. Rather, the respondent’s position
     is that it should not have to pay the applicant’s invoices because of the added
     expenses the poor loading and delayed pick-up caused the respondent.

16. I find it is clear the respondent noted on the bill of lading that Shipment #1 was
     packed improperly when it was delivered, which I accept caused additional labour
     charges. It is undisputed that Shipment #2 was not picked up on time, something
     that was known to the applicant at the time. I accept that the late pick-up caused
     additional labour and storage charges.

17. On July 31, 2017, the respondent asked the applicant for a quote on another
     project, which the applicant says is a sign that the respondent was happy with its
     service. I find nothing turns on this request, as this dispute is about Shipments #1
     and #2, and the evidence supporting each party’s position.

18. On November 7, 2017, the respondent advised the applicant it was refusing to pay
     both freight bills on both shipments. Contrary to the applicant’s submission, I find
     that the fact that Mr. Bozzer responded pleasantly is also not determinative.

19. Later on November 9, 2017, Mr. Miao wrote that he understood there were “some
     issues” with the shipment, and that “if there was something to be discussed, we
     would have needed to discuss this earlier, when I can go back to my carrier and
     have them discount the freight charges”. I find that the fact that the applicant may
     have a process with its sub-contracted carrier is not an issue for the respondent.



                                             5
20. The applicant relies upon its 13-page Terms & Conditions sheet (terms), which the
     respondent received when it applied for credit with the applicant. I accept that
     these terms apply to the parties’ agreement, which is not particularly disputed. The
     applicant says that the terms state that the customer (the respondent) must notify
     the company in writing of any disputes about invoices within 30 days (clause 5.g),
     and the applicant says that the respondent failed to do so. The terms state that the
     customer will be deemed to have accepted the invoice in full and waives any and
     all claims or defences to paying the invoice after that 30-day period.

21. If the respondent had complied with the terms and disputed the applicant’s
     invoices within 30 days, I would have likely found in favour of the respondent.
     However, the respondent did not dispute the invoices until November 9, 2017, well
     after the 30-day period. The applicant relies upon the terms when it pays its
     carriers. Given the respondent failed to give notice of its dispute with the
     applicant’s invoices within 30 days, I find the respondent has under its contract lost
     the ability to contest the invoices.

22. I do make one exception. I find the applicant cannot reasonably charge the
     respondent anything for an “after hours pick up charge”, given shipment #2 was
     admittedly not picked up until 2 p.m., through no fault of the respondent. I therefore
     deduct $400 plus $20 GST from the applicant’s invoice for shipment #2. With that
     $420 deduction, I find the respondent must pay the applicant’s invoices, in
     accordance with the terms, which totals $3,360.00.

23. The applicant is entitled to contractual interest at 26.8% on the $3,360.00, from
     August 9, 2017.

24. The applicant was substantially successful. Under section 49 of the Act and the
     tribunal’s rules, I find the respondent must reimburse the applicant $175 in tribunal
     fees.

ORDERS

25. Within 14 days of the date of this decision, I order the respondent to pay the
     applicant a total of $4,467.55, broken down as follows:
      a. $3,360.00 as payment of the applicant’s invoices 1033012 and 1033013,

      b. $932.55 in pre-judgment contractual interest at 26.8% per annum, and

      c. $175 in tribunal fees.

26. The applicant is also entitled to post-judgment interest under the Court Order
     Interest Act, as applicable.

27. Under section 48 of the Act, the tribunal will not provide the parties with the Order
     giving final effect to this decision until the time for making a notice of objection
     under section 56.1(2) has expired and no notice of objection has been made. The
     time for filing a notice of objection is 28 days after the party receives notice of the
     tribunal’s final decision.

28. Under section 58.1 of the Act, a validated copy of the tribunal’s order can be
     enforced through the Provincial Court of British Columbia. A tribunal order can
     only be enforced if it is an approved consent resolution order, or, if no objection
     has been made and the time for filing a notice of objection has passed. Once filed,
     a tribunal order has the same force and effect as an order of the Provincial Court
     of British Columbia.
