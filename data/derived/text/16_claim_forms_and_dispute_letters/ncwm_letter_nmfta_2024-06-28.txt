              RE: Uniform Shipping Law Proposal – OTH 24.1
       Dear Mr. Onwiler,
               I am writing on behalf of the National Motor Freight Traffic Association, Inc. (NMFTA) to
       express the concerns of our member motor carriers regarding the Uniform Shipping Law, OTH
       24.1, drafted by the New Hampshire Division of Weights and Measures, which is currently under
       review by the Laws and Regulations Committee of the National Conference on Weights and
       Measures (NCWM). OTH 24.1 would give states the authority to mandate standard procedures
       for carriers to incorporate into their weighing and research programs (known as W&R programs),
       and to impose penalties on carriers who fail to implement those procedures. The proposed law
       seems to be based upon the assumption that the imposition of fraudulent overcharges is a
       widespread problem in the trucking industry, even though evidence of any pattern or practice of
       such errors is lacking.
               NMFTA is a nonprofit organization with a membership comprised of motor carriers that
       primarily specialize in the transportation of less-than-truckload (LTL) quantities of freight. LTL
       transportation involves the movement of shipments that do not require all the available space
       within an entire trailer. To move these smaller freight shipments, LTL carriers consolidate goods
       tendered by many shippers into a single trailer. The freight moved is typically brought to a terminal
       by local pickup and delivery drivers, where it may be inspected, weighed and measured before it
       is sorted and consolidated with other freight heading to the same destination. Accordingly,
       NMFTA’s member carriers would be directly affected by OTH 24.1 if it is adopted in any states.
               NMFTA’s members move freight in interstate commerce throughout the United States and
       in foreign commerce between the United States and its neighbors, Canada and Mexico. Given the
       broad geographic scope of these operations, these LTL operations are largely regulated at the
       federal level: the role of states is rather limited. Indeed, any form of state regulation “related to a
       price, route, or service of any motor carrier…or…broker,” is expressly preempted by 49 U.S.C. §
       14501(c)(1). Further, states cannot legally adopt regulatory requirements that impose an “undue
       burden” on federally-regulated interstate commerce. A patchwork of unique state laws and
       regulations could easily cripple the supply chain, impeding and placing an undue burden on the
       interstate shipping process that would ultimately increase prices for both shippers and the ultimate

               Nor is state regulation of W&R programs needed to correct any significant problem in the
       LTL trucking industry. The primary purpose of the carriers’ W&R programs is to ensure that
       freight is accurately described on bills of lading, as is required by law, so the commodity classes
       can be correctly determined and the applicable pricing applied. Federal law, at 49 U.S.C. §
       80113(a), requires carriers to issue bills of lading that not only identify the parties, origin, and
       destination of each shipment, but that correctly describe the freight, the number of packages, the
       gross weight of the shipment, and the measurements if such information is needed for classification
       purposes. If the freight is subject to the NMFTA’s National Motor Freight Classification (NMFC),
       which most LTL freight is, then the description must track the item description in the NMFC, and
       include the weight and NMFC Class. See NMFC, Item 250100, Section 2(c).
               The bills of lading provided by shippers often contain incomplete or inaccurate commodity
       descriptions, weights, and dimensions of the cargo. Sometimes one or more of these essential data
       points are missing entirely. Indeed, shippers tend to rely upon the carriers to provide legally
       compliant descriptions, weight and dimensions, because they lack the proper equipment, time,
       personnel, or other resources needed to collect the information on their own. Shippers focus on
       manufacturing and selling goods, then getting those goods out the door to customers, not on
       providing complete information as required by law. New Hampshire’s proposal doesn’t seem to
       take into account the shippers’ reliance on the W&R programs to fill that gap.

               Such reliance upon carriers is well-placed. These LTL carriers typically have scales on
       their forklifts that are calibrated to accurately weigh cargo, and many have dimensioners
       programmed to collect accurate measurements. The carriers also have inspectors on staff with
       specialized training that allows them to accurately describe freight consistent with the NMFC or
       other applicable tariffs. The carriers use all these resources in their W&R programs to achieve the
       goal sought by OTH 24.1, “to ensure the accurate shipment of goods.”

                Promoting accuracy through W&R programs enables LTL carriers to make best use of
       trailer space, minimize their carbon footprint, ensure compliance with mandatory state weight
       limits, and handle and stow the goods in a manner that results in their safe and secure transport as
       mandated by federal regulations. It also helps carriers explain to shippers how and why their
       shipment was classified and priced as it was. When shippers understand the factors that go into
       correct classification and pricing, they can better describe their freight and assess their future
       shipping costs more accurately. NMFTA is actively training and educating shippers on the NMFC,
       to help them become more active stewards of the logistics relationship.

               Moreover, a shipper that believes carriers have made adjustments to bills of lading that
       resulted in unjustified charges has several potential courses of action for pursuing the matter.
       NMFTA has interpretations experts on the staff of its Freight Classification Development Council
       (FCDC) who are available to help the public by issuing opinions on classification-related issues.
       Such interpretations can be persuasive when shippers and carriers are working through differences
