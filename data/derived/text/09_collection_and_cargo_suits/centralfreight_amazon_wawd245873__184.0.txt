1    I.      INTRODUCTION AND RELIEF REQUESTED
2            Defendant Amazon Fulfillment Services (“Amazon”) offers the expert testimony of

3    William E. Partin, of Mueller & Partin, PS, Inc. (“Partin”) regarding the analysis of freight

4    overcharges Amazon withheld from Plaintiff Central Freight Lines, Inc. (“CFL”). CFL seeks to

5    bar certain analysis and conclusions set forth in Partin’s May 2, 2019 report (“Report”), as well

6    as any testimony he may offer related to that analysis. 1

7            Certain of Partin’s methods and analysis do not comport with the legal requirements of an

8    expert witness and some of his conclusions are misleading. Specifically, CFL seeks to exclude

9    Partin’s analysis and testimony on the following three matters: (i) invoices sent 180 days after

10   the shipment date; (ii) audited shipments containing between 1 and 7 pallets; and (iii) invoices

11   containing an invalid Tender ID.

12           Mr. Partin misapprehends the data he relied upon in conducting his analysis and failed to

13   review and analyze data in Amazon’s possession that is relevant to his analysis. These methods

14   and analysis render certain of his testimony inadmissible under Rule 702 of the Federal Rules of

15   Evidence. Moreover, many of his conclusions are irrelevant to the issues in this case and could

16   mislead the fact finder, in contravention of Rules 402 and 403 of the Federal Rules of Evidence.

17           Pursuant to Rules 402, 403 and 702 of the Federal Rules of Evidence and Daubert v.

18   Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 113 S.Ct. 2786 (1993), CFL moves this Court to
19   exclude certain testimony of Amazon’s expert, William E. Partin at trial and on consideration of

20   Amazon’s Motion for Partial Summary Judgment.

21   II.     LEGAL STANDARD

1    considering expert testimony offered pursuant to Federal Rule of Evidence 702, the trial court

2    acts as a “gatekeeper” by “making a preliminary determination that the expert’s testimony is

3    reliable.” Elsayed Mukhtar v. Cal. State Univ., Hayward, 299 F.3d 1053, 1063 (9th Cir. 2002);

4    see Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137, 147–48 (1999); Gen. Elec. Co. v. Joiner, 522

5    U.S. 136, 142 (1997); Daubert, 509 U.S. at 589–90. “To fulfill its gate-keeping role, the court

6    must strike the appropriate balance between admitting reliable, helpful expert testimony and

7    excluding misleading or confusing testimony.” United States v. Rincon, 28 F.3d 921, 926 (9th

8    Cir. 1994).

9           An expert witness may provide opinion testimony if: (1) the testimony is based upon

10   sufficient facts or data; (2) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and methods; and

11   (3) the expert has reliably applied the principles and methods to the facts of the case. Fed. R.

12   Evid. 702; United States v. W.R. Grace, 504 F.3d 745, 765 (9th Cir. 2007). Nevertheless,

13   depending on the type of expert testimony offered, these factors may not be appropriate to assess

14   reliability. Kumho Tire, 526 U.S. at 150, 119 S.Ct. 1167. Other factors that might be considered

15   include whether an expert has unjustifiably extrapolated from an accepted premise to an

16   unfounded conclusion, see General Elec. Co. v. Joiner, 522 U.S. 136, 146, 118 S.Ct. 512 (1997);

17   or whether an expert has adequately accounted for obvious alternative explanations, see Claar v.

18   Burlington Northern R. Co., 29 F.3d 499, 502 (9th Cir. 1994).

1            As has been set forth in previous filings, after CFL refused to pay Amazon the sums set

2    forth Amazon’s August 10, 2016 Demand Letter, Amazon implemented a scheme to “set off” the

3    money CFL refused to pay. Pursuant to this scheme, Amazon purposefully built up a significant

4    balance of money owed to CFL for services provided, and then willfully refused to pay invoices

5    totaling over $2.8 million. In his Report, Partin offers an analysis that purports to show that some

6    CFL’s outstanding invoices that were part of the setoff were sent to Amazon more than 180 days

7    after the date of the shipment. (Report at Page 4, Attachment 2 and M&P Setoff Analysis.xlsx.)

8           Section 2.2 of the Transportation Agreement between Amazon and CFL provides,

9    “Amazon has no obligation to pay any fees or expenses invoiced more than 6 months after they

10   accrue (“Stale Fees”).” (Dkt. # 139-1 at 3.) CFL anticipates that Amazon will attempt to use

11   Partin’s analysis of the timeliness of invoices sent to Amazon at trial to bar a portion of CFL’s

12   claims on outstanding invoices, arguing that some were sent more than 6 months – 180 days –

13   after the shipment date and are thus ineligible for payment.

14          Partin’s Report shows that CFL sent 295 invoices to Amazon more than 180 days after

15   the shipment date (See Report at Attachment 2 (266 + 29 = 295).) However, had Partin reviewed

16   and analyzed the proper data, his analysis would have shown that the vast majority of these 295

17   invoices were sent to Amazon within 180 days of the shipment date.

9            Daubert does not permit an expert to present testimony or conclusions based upon such

10   unreliable speculation as evidence at trial. For instance, in Gen. Elec. Co. v. Joiner, the Court

11   held it was not an abuse of discretion for the trial court to exclude expert opinion testimony that

12   was premised on studies that the court found “were not a sufficient basis for the experts’

13   opinions.” Gen. Elec., 522 U.S. 136, 146 (1997). The Court held that trial courts may properly

14   examine how trained experts extrapolate from existing data and noted that “nothing in either

15   Daubert or the Federal Rules of Evidence requires a district court to admit opinion evidence that

16   is connected to existing data only by the ipse dixit of the expert. A court may conclude that there

17   is simply too great an analytical gap between the data and the opinion proffered.” Id.

18           Here, Partin simply guessed that the “Invoice Date” referred to the original invoice date.
19   This renders his testimony on this issue inadmissible because it is not based upon sufficient facts

20   or data and because his guess, which was wrong, establishes that his testimony on this issue

21   would be unreliable, as an analysis of the correct data would have would have shown that the
