## 1. Stage map

### S0: set objectives, constraints and team fit; fix knock-out criteria and weights before looking at candidates

- **Fix criteria and weights in advance (Dolnicar Step 2 → Step 8), R14.**
  - Steps: in Step 2 the team agrees knock-out criteria and attractiveness criteria with weights (percentages summing to 100%). Step 8 only re-checks and applies them.
  - Rationale: "there is no single best measure of segment attractiveness or relative organisational competitiveness. It is therefore necessary for users to return to their specifications of what an ideal target segment looks like for them." *(verified in R14 note)*
  - Evidence: textbook authority.
  - Adaptation *(reader inference)*: the project's three CLAUDE.md constraints (software-improvable, measurable cost, fee as a share of savings) are exactly the kind of knock-outs R14 says must be fixed before candidates are seen. Writing weights down before scoring stops a favourite, such as the farmer lead, from getting weights tuned to fit it.
- **Team-weighted criteria to beat "who shouts the loudest" (DPM), R12.**
  - Steps: senior team selects the variables; "each should 'vote' with 100 points, then the whole team's votes should be aggregated". *(verified)*
  - Rationale: "Often the 'who shouts the loudest' approach to management meetings wins, or else historical successes with specific clients, products or markets colour the judgement of decision-makers irrespective of current business performance." *(verified)*
  - Evidence: authority + one case.
- **Personal filter first (Aulet), R15, R16.**
  - Steps: before any market analysis ask "Is this market segment one I could get really, really excited about and dedicate the next six years of my life to serving? ... If not, don't do it." *(verified, R15 note)* Step 2 ends with the whole team signing an "Agreement on the Beachhead Market Selection". *(verified, R16)*
  - Rationale: "in my experience, failure to address team issues upfront is the number one reason companies fail." *(verified, R16)*
  - Evidence: author experience only.
- **Start from core abilities (Where to Play Step 1), R40.** "Your core abilities or technologies can create different offerings for different types of customers." *(verified)* *Reader inference:* our core ability is software/automation capability plus whatever freight access the team has.
- **Prior knowledge shapes what you can see (Shane), R36.** "entrepreneurs discover opportunities related to the information that they already possess" and "entrepreneurs do not always select between alternative market opportunities". *(verified: abstract)* Evidence: 8 case studies. *Reader inference:* make "team access and insider knowledge" an explicit S0 input and S4 criterion, and plan to buy or build it where it is missing.
- **Company objectives and resources (Kotler's third evaluation factor), R09.** *(secondary, unverified)*. Also Fripp's "synergy with existing capabilities" (R01, verified in note), which is empty for a pre-product team *(reader inference)*.
- **Fix the definition frame before needs discovery (Ulwick), R18.** "the market must be defined and validated in the innovation equation before moving to needs discovery and product definition." *(verified)* This fixes the frame (who plus which job), not the choice.
- **Convergence:** R12, R14 and R15/R16 all fix criteria (R12/R14 also fix weights) before scoring. R15, R16, R36 and R40 all make team knowledge or fit a starting input. That is strong support for an explicit S0 step whose output is a written criteria-and-weights sheet.
- **Divergence:** Aulet puts team fit first, as a gate before market analysis (R15). Dolnicar and the DPM fold organisational fit into one scored axis (R14, R12). Kotler makes it the third of three evaluation factors (R09). See §4 T3.

### S1: define the market and enumerate candidate sub-markets

- **Abell's three axes, R05/R06.**
  - Candidates are cells of customer groups (WHO) × customer functions (WHAT) × technologies (HOW), written as "providing CF1, CF2 and CF3 to / for: CG1, CG2, CG3 and CG4 using: T1 and T2". *(verified, R06)*
  - Rationale: "products are merely a physical manifestation of the application of a particular technology to the satisfaction of a particular function for a particular customer group. The choice is one of technologies, functions and customers to serve." *(verified)*
  - Limitation: "does not provide assistance to determine the appropriate scale or size of a business" *(verified)*, so the axes enumerate but do not choose.
  - Adaptation *(reader inference)*: shipper groups on the customer axis, shipping jobs on the function axis, freight-company type or method (asset carrier, broker, 3PL, forwarder, in-house fleet, spreadsheet/phone, software) on the "how" axis. The project's two-sided definition is an Abell cell.
- **Purpose-specific definitions (Day), R07.**
  - Top-down definitions ("competitive capabilities and resource transferability") and bottom-up definitions ("customer requirements or usage patterns"); "different market definitions are needed for different strategic purposes"; a "strategically relevant balance of cost and demand factors". *(verified: abstract)*
  - Adaptation *(reader inference)*: use the bottom-up (shipper usage) definition for problem discovery. Use the top-down (shared software capability, data and integrations) definition for ability-to-win and expansion.
- **Jobs-to-be-done definition (Ulwick), R18.**
  - A market is "a group of people and the job they are trying to get done". The 8-step canvas runs: product → job executors (not "influencers, economic buyers") → abstracted executor → final executor → product function → adjacent products → abstracted job → final job. Steps 5-8 are done through customer interviews. *(verified)*
  - Eight quality tests: constant, stable over time, unique, "does not assume a product or a solution", identifies the targets, makes needs discovery cheaper, "reveals all sources of competition", aligns the organisation. *(verified)*
  - Two-sided: "it can be used twice to define both sides of a double-sided market" and "for each constituent in the distribution chain". *(verified)*
- **Sub-market vocabulary and checks (Fripp), R01/R02/R03.**
  - Layering: "you first identify sub-markets ..., then you define where you plan to compete ..., and then segment that sub-market". *(verified, R01 note)*
  - Five-item sub-market test: "Unique product features or attributes, Distinct customer groupings or usage contexts, Specialized competitors ..., Different price structures or cost factors, Tailored marketing or distribution methods". *(verified, R02 note)*
  - Product/market grid: rows are products, columns are (sub-)markets, and gaps are "unserved markets". *(verified, R02)*
  - Four bounding approaches: NAICS, product category, geography, directories. NAICS caveat: it "can also be limiting if it overlooks overlaps between industries or if a firm's product cuts across multiple classifications". *(verified, R03 note)*
  - The warning: "Sub-markets revolve around product categories, whereas market segments revolve around customer needs or behaviors". *(verified, R01)*
- **B2B segmentation variables, nested (Shapiro & Bonoma), R08.**
  - "Often the same industrial products have multiple applications; likewise, several different products can be used in the same application." *(verified)*
  - Nests run from outer and observable (demographics, operating variables; verified in the monograph TOC) to inner (purchasing approach, situational factors, personal characteristics; *secondary, unverified*).
  - *Reader inference:* screen sub-markets on the outer nests using public data, and leave the inner nests to interviews and the segment stage.
- **Wide brainstorm (Aulet 1A), R15.** "focus on getting as long a list as possible ... Let a thousand flowers bloom!"; "some of the most interesting ... Beachhead Markets ... exist on the fringe"; "open the aperture as far as possible". Market-pull dimension grid (Gradeable): customise the dimensions to the problem. *(verified, R15 note)*
- **Market Opportunity Set (Where to Play Step 1), R40.**
  - "A varied set of market opportunities is an asset in and of itself ... It also provides the basis for plan-B"; "Get a wide-lens perspective beyond your industry boundaries". *(verified)*
  - Flyability example: the first idea (disaster observation) broadened to "five market domains". *(verified, Blank)*
- **Market mapping (McDonald & Dunbar), R11.** Index terms "market map", "junction types", "final users", "Distributors" are *verified*. The method (tracing flows and the junctions where buying decisions sit) is *reader knowledge, unverified*.
- **Industry scope (Porter), R43.** *Reader knowledge, unverified:* bound the relevant industry by product and geographic scope, splitting areas with different structures. *Verified (ISC):* "There may be multiple buyer segments in a given industry with different levels of power."
- **Output of S1** *(reader inference)*: a long list (tens) of candidates, each written in one comparable sentence:
  - Template: "[shipping job] for [shipper group] done today by [freight-company type/method]".
  - Each candidate gets a demand-side label (who plus job, R18) and a supply-side label (how, R06).
- **Convergence:** R05/R06, R18, R03 (definition fixes the segmentation axis) and R07's bottom-up view all push toward defining by customer and job rather than product category. R15 and R40 both push wide enumeration before narrowing. R08 and R03 both warn that one supply-side taxonomy cannot capture B2B customer differences.
- **Divergence:** R01/R02 frame sub-markets as product categories (supply side). R18 forbids defining a market by product. R07 says both are needed for different purposes. See §4 T6.

### S2: knock-out screen (pass/fail viability tests)

- **ADAMS (R10; the textbook ancestor is R09).** Accessible ("reach ... at an affordable cost"), Differentiable ("internally homogeneous ... externally heterogeneous"), Actionable ("practical (or profitable) ... outcomes ... that can be easily quantified"), Measurable ("accurately estimate the size ... in terms of either sales value or number of customers"), Substantial ("a group too small to justify the expenditure of resources"). *(verified)* No thresholds are given.
- **Dolnicar's six knock-outs, R14:** "homogeneity, distinctness, size, match, identifiability and reachability", where match means needs the organisation can satisfy. They are fixed in Step 2 and re-checked in Step 8 ("the knock-out criteria have already been applied in previous steps"). *(verified, R14 note)*
- **Aulet's three conditions for "a market", R16.**
  - The conditions: "Same product", "Same sales process" ("interchangeable language, value proposition, sales channels"), and "Word of mouth". Worksheet 02.1 adds "actual human end users and not just general companies" and a size band "less than a billion and more than a few hundred thousand" (units unstated). If a candidate fails, "continue to iterate on the market segmentation matrix until they are". *(verified)*
  - The worksheet scale's "Show Stopper" level works as an embedded knock-out.
- **Fripp's five-item sub-market test (R02):** distinct on products, customers or usage, competitors, prices or costs, and channels. *(verified in note)*
- **Cohen's multiplicative zero (R19):** Eager-identity "0: They cannot buy from you" zeroes the whole product score, because the rows are "a series of 'ands'". *(verified in note)*
- **Rationale for a separate, cheap gate:** "primary market research is time-consuming and expensive in terms of opportunity costs, so you want to focus your research on compelling candidates" (R16). R04 separates "Evaluate Segment Viability" (size, accessibility, alignment) from "Evaluate Attractiveness" (profitability, competitiveness). *(verified)*
- **Adaptation *(reader inference)*:**
  - Project knock-outs: (a) the cost is software-addressable, (b) the cost pool is measurable per customer, (c) a fee as a share of savings is feasible, meaning savings are attributable and someone can pay (link to Agent B's CAMP test, R25).
  - Apply R16's "same product / same sales process" to both sides. A candidate that needs different products for its shippers and its carriers may be two sub-markets.
- **Convergence:** size or substance (R10, R14, R16), reachability or accessibility (R10, R14, R15/R16) and homogeneity or distinctness (R10, R14, R16, R02) appear in three or more sources each. These are the core knock-outs.
- **Divergence:** Dolnicar's "match" is a knock-out, while Aulet scores "whole product" rather than gating on it. Word of mouth appears only in Aulet (and implicitly in Moore's references, R17). "Human end users" (R16) conflicts with company-level freight candidates *(reader inference)*: it has to be restated as the named role, e.g. the logistics coordinator or dispatcher.

### S3: desk evidence (size, growth, structure, profit pools)

This half is thin on data methods; Agent B holds the datasets and vertical-SaaS sizing (R20, R31-R34, R42, R44, R45).

- **Size and growth plus structural attractiveness (Kotler), R09** *(secondary, unverified)*: "The largest, fastest-growing segments are not always the most attractive ones for every company."
- **Five forces as the structural diagnostic, R43** *(verified, ISC)*:
  - Buyer power is "highest when buyers are large relative to the competitors serving them, products are undifferentiated and represent a significant cost for the buyer, and there are few switching costs".
  - Rivalry is fierce when "Competitors are numerous or are roughly equal in size", "Industry growth is slow", "high fixed costs", "Exit barriers are high".
  - Substitutes: "the same underlying need ... in a different way".
  - "Industry structure is dynamic".
- **Top-down vs bottom-up sizing (Fripp), R03:** "a precise definition of what market you are measuring remains the foundation for accurate sizing". *(verified in note)*
- **Fermi estimates from quick searches (Cohen), R19:** power-of-ten counts ("page-one Google search results"), "to avoid the analysis-paralysis of deep research". *(verified in note)*
- **Order-of-magnitude rows (Aulet's matrix), R15:** "Size of Market (# of End Users)" and "Est. Value of End User ($1, $10, $100, $1K, etc.)". *(verified in note)*
- **Criteria named by Simkin & Dibb, R13:** "Market size, growth rates, competitive forces, customer fit and profitability". *(verified: abstract)*
- **Criteria balance rule (DPM), R12:** a mix of short-term (sales volumes, current profitability) and long-term (growth prospects, "ability to sustain a differential advantage") criteria, and of internal-facing and market-facing criteria. *(verified)*
- **Limit on desk research (Aulet), R15:** "If there is already a market research report out there with all the information you need, it is probably too late for your new venture." "Somewhere around 90% of the data in the chart came from direct interaction". *(verified in note)*
- **Adaptation *(reader inference)*:** desk evidence is for knock-outs and for cutting the long list to a shortlist of 4-10, not for the final choice.
  - Size the savings pool (avoidable cost per shipment × volume), not freight spend. R03's sizing paragraph measures revenue, the wrong currency for the fee rule.
  - Run the five forces twice: once on the customer's industry (who keeps the saving) and once on our software market.
- **Convergence:** size, growth, competition and profitability recur across R09, R12, R13 and R43.
- **Divergence:** Aulet distrusts secondary data for new markets (R15). Cohen and Fripp rely on it for first estimates (R19, R03). See T5.

### S4: score and rank (attractiveness to us × our ability to win)

- **DPM / GE grid (R12; R11 is the book behind it).**
  - Steps (1)-(5): balanced attractiveness criteria; 100-point weights; strength criteria with 100-point weights; score each candidate "n x 1 = strong/good; n x 0.5 = 'so-so'/average; n x 0 = low/ weak/poor"; weight × score gives 0-100 per axis; plot attractiveness on Y and strength on X.
  - Strength is scored "relative to the dominant player(s) in the market" and attractiveness "one market segment versus 'the others'". Bubble = "proportionate income"; the team also projects "where the segments would head over the following three years".
  - The real case used 11 attractiveness and 10 strength criteria (weights such as 14 and 8). *(verified)*
- **Segment evaluation plot (Dolnicar Step 8), R14.**
  - Ratings are 1-10 per criterion and weights are percentages. X axis "How attractive is the segment to us?"; Y axis "How attractive are we to the segment?"; bubble = "Typically profit potential".
  - Then: compatibility check if targeting more than one segment; "Present the selected segments to the advisory committee"; the plot "serves as a useful basis for discussions in the segmentation team". The "Would you like to marry this person? ... Would this person marry you?" analogy frames the two axes. *(verified in note)*
- **Attractiveness Map (Where to Play Step 2), R40.**
  - Axes are "value creation potential" and "overall challenge in capturing this value". Purpose: "the basis for team discussions so that you are less susceptible to biases and intuition". *(verified)*
  - Sub-criteria gated: *reader knowledge, unverified*: potential = compelling value, market size, growth, economic viability; challenge = implementation obstacles, time to revenue, external funding needs, competitive threat.
- **Aulet's beachhead worksheet (R16) and Step 1B vote (R15).** Eight criteria are rated "Very High (best - 1), High, Medium, Low, Show Stopper (worst - 4)", with rows "Overall rating", "Ranking" and "Key deciding factors". There are no weights; the choice comes from discussion. In Step 1B: "have the group vote on the remaining market segments to get a prioritized list". *(verified)*
- **Cohen's multiplicative rubric, R19.**
  - Seven rows (Plausible, Self-Aware, Lucrative, Liquid, Eager ×2, Enduring) are scored on power-of-ten or 0.01-1.0 scales, multiplied, and divided by 625,000. A score of 1 means a viable indie business; 2 or more means a scale-up.
  - Rationale: "Now you multiply. Why multiply? Because this is a series of 'ands' ... The effects compound."
  - Caution: "This is dangerously close to a silly quiz ... having to think through the answers and trade-offs is most of the value of the exercise". *(verified in note)*
  - Unit of analysis: "a specific type of buyer solving a specific problem with a product that has made specific trade-offs, at a specific price."
- **Kotler's three factors, R09** *(secondary, unverified)*: size and growth; structural attractiveness; objectives and resources. They map onto attractiveness (first two) and fit (third).
- **Moore's compact rule, R17** *(verified in podcast secondary)*: "big enough to matter, small enough to lead, and a good fit with your crown jewels".
- **What practice actually does (R13):** "many businesses continue to assess target markets on the basis of short‐term profitability measures"; "formal, multi‐criteria tools ... are not necessarily reflected in the simplistic view adopted by many marketing professionals". *(verified: abstract)*
- **Evidence base for S4 overall:** mostly authority. R12 has one real case. R13 shows the tools are under-used in practice but does not show that they perform better. R40 cites research on portfolio generation (85 VC-backed startups, via Blank) and has its own 25-venture study on entry strategy, not on scoring accuracy. No source in this half shows that weighted scoring predicts venture success *(reader inference)*.
- **Adaptation *(reader inference)*:**
  - Use two additive weighted axes (R12/R14 mechanics), with our own criteria:
    - Attractiveness to us: savings pool, software-addressability, measurability and attribution of savings, ability to pay and who keeps the saving, growth or durability, leverage to adjacent sub-markets.
    - Ability to win: access and word of mouth, team knowledge, whole-product feasibility and time-to-value, incumbent substitutes and competition, buying liquidity.
  - Bubble = fee potential (savings pool × plausible share).
  - Add two checks: Cohen's multiplicative score, to catch a weak conjunctive link; and R16's "Show Stopper" flag.
  - Record the "Key deciding factors" (R16) so the choice can be audited later, as CLAUDE.md requires.
- **Convergence:** three independent sources (R12, R14, R40) plus Kotler (R09) use two axes: how good the market is versus how well-placed we are, or how hard it is to capture. Two (R12, R14) use weighted sums set by the team. All treat the output as input to team discussion, not as the verdict (R12 "objectivity has an input", R14 "basis for discussions", R40 "basis for team discussions", R19 "guidance rather than precise analysis").
- **Divergence:** see T7 (additive vs multiplicative vs ordinal) and T4 (tools vs practice). The second axis also differs in meaning: relative strength against dominant players (R12), how attractive we are to the segment (R14), and challenge in capturing value (R40).

### S5: problem discovery and validation before commitment

- **Primary market research to fill the comparison matrix (Aulet), R15, R16.**
  - Modes: "speaking with them (i.e., interviewing), watching them as they work (i.e., primary observational research), doing the job yourself (i.e., immersion), seeing their responses to carefully designed tests (i.e., A/B testing), or following their digital dust".
  - Posture: "inquiry mode", not "advocacy mode".
  - Time: "at least a few weeks"; SensAble spent "over three months"; "Somewhere around 90% of the data in the chart came from direct interaction".
  - Keep doing PMR during Step 2 (R16). The 17-row matrix includes Task, Urgency of Need, Willingness to Change, Concentration of Buyers, Competition/Alternatives ("Include the 'do nothing' option"), Other Components Needed and Important Partners. Log format: Hypothesis / Validated / Finding / Next Step (Bloom case). *(verified in note)*
- **Evidence-seeking interviews (Mom Test), R35.**
  - "You shouldn't ask anyone if your business is a good idea. It's a bad question and everyone will lie to you at least a little." *(verified: official blurb)*
  - Rules: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past; talk less *(secondary, unverified)*.
  - "Customer slicing" to a reachable "who-where pair" *(secondary, unverified)*.
  - Commitments count as evidence *(reader knowledge, unverified)*.
- **JTBD interviews (Ulwick), R18.** "complete steps 5–8 employing customer interviews"; define the job in the executors' own words; ask what "other products they use immediately before, while, and immediately after". *(verified)*
- **Insider information (Shane), R36.** Opportunities come from information already held *(verified: abstract)*. *Reader inference:* immersion or insider advisors are needed to see a sub-market's problems.
- **Problem ≠ business (Cohen), R19.**
  - The failure pattern: "Founder talks to three potential customers ... They agree The Problem exists ... fails to make enough sales". *(verified in note)*
  - Research questions: Self-Aware ("Do they know & care"), Lucrative ("annual allocated budget"; "target companies who outsource this particular problem to outside vendors"), Liquid (timing, contracts, lock-in).
- **Compelling reason to buy (Moore), R17:** "addressing a critical problem or pain point" *(verified in secondary)*. Pragmatists want proof "in use cases that they themselves have and with customers they know and can reference" *(verified)*.
- **Where the Navigator sits (Where to Play), R40:** it is "the front-end of Customer Development": first choose where to search, then run customer discovery there *(verified, Blank)*.
- **Adaptation *(reader inference)*:** run two tiers.
  - S5a, before the final choice: short structured interviews per shortlisted candidate, on both sides. Ask past-behaviour questions about the cost incidents that would form the savings baseline (claims, detention, accessorials, invoice errors, empty miles, tender rejections). Count commitments, such as sharing invoices or TMS exports, as the evidence.
  - S5b, after the choice: deep discovery in the chosen sub-market (segment stage, Q6).
- **Convergence:** R15, R18, R35 and R17 all require direct contact with the customer before committing. R15 and R36 both imply that desk data will miss the real problems.
- **Divergence:** R15 wants primary research across 4-10 candidates before choosing. R40 (via Blank) positions deep customer development after choosing. R18 wants the market defined before needs discovery. See T8 for the resolution.

### S6: choose a beachhead plus backups; wedge; expansion path

- **One beachhead, keep segmenting (Aulet), R16.**
  - "Choose a single market segment to pursue; then, keep segmenting until you have a well-deﬁned and homogenous market opportunity ... Focus is your ally."
  - Use the eight criteria, including "Can you leverage this market segment to enter others?" (R16; R15 cites Moore's bowling-alley lead pin).
  - "burn the boats! Pursue it until it proves not to work or is much less attractive than originally anticipated." *(verified)*
- **Single use case in a single industry (Moore), R17:** "targets a single, highly challenging use case in a single industry and just nails it", to become "the clear leader in its chosen niche". *(verified)* "Big enough to matter, small enough to lead" *(verified in secondary)*. Four sequential playbooks; "combining playbooks can actually undermine success" *(verified in secondary)*.
- **Agile Focus (Where to Play Step 3), R40.**
  - Define one primary opportunity, "Design a smart portfolio of backup and growth options to keep open", "Say 'no' to all other options". *(verified)*
  - Evidence (Tal, Gruber and de Haan working paper, 25 ventures): "72% ... had to adapt their initial market entry decision during the first three years". Broad-approach ventures raised on average $7.38M; 18% sought more funds at year three versus 66% (focused) and 60% (vague); headcount 18.8 vs 5.75 vs 4.6. The broad approach's alternatives "tend to be closely related". *(verified)*
- **Concentrated marketing for resource-poor firms (Kotler/OpenStax), R06, R09.** "Advisable when the company resources are small ... And/or at an early stage of the product life cycle" (R06, verified). "a smart strategy for smaller companies or companies with limited resources" (OpenStax §5.5, verified; R09 note).
- **Niche re-targeting (Cohen), R19:** "targeting a niche often results in a better business model, because although it reduces the number of target customers, it can increase several other numbers ... the niche is your way to get started, and you expand the target market over time." *(verified in note)*
- **Compatibility and a second segment (Dolnicar), R14:** if targeting more than one segment, "make sure that the selected target segments are compatible with one another". In the worked plot, the best match may be small, so a second segment that "loves the organisation" is added. *(verified in note)*
- **Moderate breadth (Fripp), R03:** "Start with a moderate definition ... Revise the market definition if ... justify a broader or different lens." *(verified in note)*
- **Adaptation *(reader inference)*:** commit execution to one primary sub-market. Keep 1-2 documented backups and 1-2 growth options that share the same software core, data sources and one side of the market, e.g. the same shipper group on a second mode, or the same carrier type in an adjacent commodity. Then "switching" re-uses research and product (R40's "closely related" alternatives).
- **Convergence:** R16, R17, R40, R06/R09 and R19 all say start in one narrow, winnable place. This is the strongest agreement in the half.
- **Divergence:** whether alternatives stay open. "Burn the boats" (R16) and single-playbook focus (R17) against Agile Focus backed by evidence (R40). See T1.

### S7: record the decision; revisit triggers; iteration

## 4. Tensions and open questions

**T1. Focus vs flexibility.**
- One side: "burn the boats!" (R16); "a single, highly challenging use case in a single industry" (R17); "combining playbooks can actually undermine success" (R17, secondary).
- Other side: "consciously keeping related options open, you can mitigate your risk and avoid a potentially fatal lock-in" (R40). In the working paper, 72% of 25 ventures adapted within three years, and broad-approach ventures outperformed (R40).
- Candidate resolution *(reader judgement)*: focus execution, including product and sales, on one primary. Keep options open only as documented, closely related backups that share the software core. Write revisit triggers into the decision record. This is consistent with both camps: Aulet also allows a return ("you can always come back"), and Where to Play also demands "Say 'no' to all other options".
- Evidence that would settle it: larger, multi-industry studies of entry breadth vs performance, or our own cost of switching between shortlisted sub-markets.

**T2. Systematic selection vs discovery from prior knowledge.**
- One side: the enumerate-score-select methods (R12, R14, R15, R40).
- Other side: "entrepreneurs do not always select between alternative market opportunities" and "discover opportunities related to the information that they already possess" (R36).
- Candidate resolution: run the selection process, but score team access and insider knowledge explicitly, and budget immersion or advisors to create insider knowledge in the leading candidates. Expect the selection to be partly a search.
- Evidence that would settle it: whether S5a interviews in unfamiliar sub-markets surface concrete, costed problems as readily as in familiar ones.

**T3. Team fit as a first gate vs as one scored factor.**
- One side: "The first question you should ask is a personal filter question" (R15).
- Other side: team fit is folded into a competitiveness axis (R14, R12) or into "company objectives and resources" (R09, unverified).
- Candidate resolution: apply it twice. First as a knock-out on candidates the team would not commit to for years; then as scored access and knowledge.
- Evidence that would settle it: none external; it is a values choice the team must make and record.

**T4. Formal multi-criteria tools vs what practitioners do.**
- One side: the R12/R14 machinery.
- Other side: practitioners assess on "short‐term profitability measures", and formal tools are "not necessarily reflected" in practice (R13). Also "dangerously close to a silly quiz" (R19).
- Candidate resolution: use scoring to structure discussion and force explicit trade-offs, which all sources agree on. Keep it coarse. Record deciding factors. Never let one short-term number, such as freight spend, decide alone.
- Evidence that would settle it: a study linking scoring discipline to venture outcomes. None was found in this half.

**T5. Secondary vs primary evidence.**
- One side: "If there is already a market research report out there with all the information you need, it is probably too late for your new venture"; ~90% primary data (R15).
- Other side: Fermi estimates from "page-one Google search results" (R19); top-down sizing (R03).
- Candidate resolution: desk evidence for S2/S3 (knock-outs and the shortlist); primary evidence for S5a and the final choice.
- Evidence that would settle it: how much S5a interviews change the desk ranking. If they rarely change it, reduce S5a.

**T6. What a "sub-market" is.**
- One side: product categories ("Sub-markets revolve around product categories", R01) and the industry's own taxonomy ("List the product or service categories typically recognized", R02).
- Other side: people plus job, not product (R18); customer groups × functions × technologies (R06); purpose-dependent (R07).
- Candidate resolution: each candidate is an Abell cell with a demand-side label (shipper group + job, R18) and a supply-side label (freight-company type / method, R02 grid rows). Validate with R02's five indicia and R16's three conditions.
- Evidence that would settle it: whether candidates defined by job produce more distinct problem lists in interviews than candidates defined by mode.

**T7. Additive vs multiplicative vs ordinal scoring.**
- The three positions: weighted sums (R12, R14); multiplication "because this is a series of 'ands'" (R19); unweighted ordinal ratings with discussion (R16).
- Candidate resolution: additive ranking on two axes, plus a multiplicative check and a show-stopper veto (see §3).
- Evidence that would settle it: sensitivity analysis on our own sheet. If the top candidate changes under reasonable weight changes, the evidence is too thin to decide.

**T8. Order of definition, research and choice.**
- R18: define the market "before moving to needs discovery". R15: fill a matrix by primary research across 4-10 candidates, then choose. R40: choose first; the Navigator is "the front-end of Customer Development".
- Candidate resolution: these fit together as a sequence. Fix the definition frame (R18) at S1. Do light primary research on the shortlist (R15) at S5a. Do deep customer development after the choice (R40) at S5b / Q6.

**T9. Size: small beachhead vs viability thresholds.**
- "not too big and not too small" (R15); "big enough to matter, small enough to lead" (R17, secondary); worksheet band "less than a billion and more than a few hundred thousand" (R16).
- Against these, Cohen's B2B plausibility threshold is ~100k businesses (R19). Freight intermediary populations may fall below it, e.g. >17,000 US brokers per R27 (Agent B's half; cited in the R19 note).
- Candidate resolution: size by fee potential (savings × share × customers) rather than head-count. Allow smaller counts where per-customer savings are large, which Cohen himself allows ("a high-price-tag product in a small niche can be a fine company", R19).

**T10. Which side to start with in a two-sided sub-market.**
- R15: "start by focusing your efforts on the more difficult side first. It is usually on the demand side, but not always."
- Fee constraint *(reader inference)*: the side whose cost falls may not be the side that pays or decides. Market-map junctions (R11, unverified) and "who keeps the saving" (R43) bear on this.
- Candidate resolution: for each candidate, record separately (a) whose cost falls, (b) who decides, (c) who pays, and (d) which side is harder to win.
- Open question: no source in this half gives a method for pricing when payer ≠ saver. Agent B's pricing sources (R23, R25) should be checked.
