## Strategies or frameworks described

### 1. The ten factors for evaluating a marketplace opportunity (the author's numbered-by-heading list)
- Origin and intended context: Benchmark's experience since eBay. Marketplaces that "reached significant scale" include OpenTable, Yelp, Zillow, oDesk, GrubHub, 1stdibs, UShip and Uber. Gurley also admits failures: "we have also invested in several companies that we thought had marketplace opportunities that simply did not play out as expected." B2C and B2B two-sided markets. *(verified)*
- Framing (verbatim): "A true marketplace needs natural pull on both the consumer and supplier side of the market. Aggregating suppliers is a necessary, but insufficient step on its own. You must also organically aggregate demand." "Highly liquid marketplaces naturally 'tip' towards becoming a clearinghouse where neither the consumer nor the supplier would favor an alternative."
- Mechanism and steps: the ten factors, with the author's key definition for each (verbatim):
  1. "New Experience vs. the Status Quo. Great marketplaces do not simply aggregate a market; they enhance it." When "the experience delta is great enough, it creates 'wow' moments for new users. 'Wow' moments lead to word-of-mouth viral growth".
  2. "Economic Advantages vs. the Status Quo." "If you can positively change the economics of an industry, you will find the participants on both sides rooting for your success."
  3. "Opportunity for Technology to Add Value." At Uber, "the system has 'perfect' information in an industry where just two years ago there was a complete lack of visibility (on both sides of the network) that led to enormous waste of resources." "Facilitating work-flow reduces work for the participants, as well as increasing switching costs."
  4. "High Fragmentation. High buyer and supplier fragmentation is a huge positive for an online marketplace. Likewise, a concentrated supplier (or purchaser) base greatly diminishes the likelihood of a successful online marketplace. A highly concentrated supplier base will be reluctant to allow a new intermediary in their market, and as a result will likely fight rather than support your arrival." Example: airlines "all but obliterated the economics of online ticketing marketplaces". Portfolio pattern: "a common theme of fragmented supplier base."
  5. "Friction of Supplier Sign-Up." Slow, high-touch signup "slows your roll-out and increases the costs associated with supplier aggregation", but "can be a barrier to entry" later. "Remember, however, that supplier aggregation is the easy part. Aggregating demand is much harder and more critical."
  6. "Size of the Market Opportunity." "if all the other factors are negative, it will not matter that the market is large." "you should also consider the percentage of the market that is likely to use the online alternative." Healthcare's oligopoly "is unlikely to support a new alternative". OpenTable's TAM was underestimated. "You must combine a TAM analysis with the likelihood of marketplace success and penetration."
  7. "Expand the Market." "certain marketplaces do indeed expand the market — by exploring new price points or enhancing convenience or usability" (oDesk, Uber).
  8. "Frequency. All things being equal, a higher frequency is obviously better." "Many failed marketplaces attack purchasing cycles that are simply way too infrequent". Also "attacking verticals where a satisfactory supplier 'match' end's the customer's need to re-enter the market" (monogamous relationships).
  9. "Payment Flow. All things being equal, being part of the payment flow is superior to not being a part of the payment flow. This is due to the fact that it is much easier to extract reasonable economics when you are in the flow of payment." By contrast, when a marketplace bills "the supplier at later date for services rendered ... it's easier for the supplier to view it is a 'tax' versus a distribution relationship. Cash is king, and if you bring the cash, you are king."
  10. "Network Effects." "Can the marketplace provide a better experience to customer 'n+1000' than it did to customer 'n' directly as a function of adding 1000 more participants to the market?" "Network effects are rare but golden."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: a qualitative judgement per factor, with an implicit binary match or no-match. Inputs are the buyer and supplier structure, transaction frequency, where the payment sits, and TAM × likely penetration.
- Output produced: a count out of 10. Scoring rule (verbatim): "It is unlikely that you will find a marketplace opportunity that would score ten out of ten with respect to this list. However a 7 or 8 out of 10 would imply that your opportunity of success is much, much higher than if you only match 3 or 4 out of 10."
- Rationale and evidence base:
  - Each factor carries its own why. Fragmentation: concentrated parties "fight rather than support your arrival". Frequency builds word of mouth. Payment flow avoids the "tax" perception.
  - The evidence is one investor's portfolio of successes and failures (cases and authority). There is no quantitative study behind the 7-8/10 rule.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques:
  - Stated: "this analysis could easily mislead one into underestimating the critical role that execution plays"; "you also need a world-class entrepreneur".
  - *Reader inference:* equal weights; 2012 vintage; marketplace-specific, so factors 5 and 10 matter less for pure SaaS. UShip, a freight marketplace, appears among his scale-reaching examples.
- Key quotes: see above.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:*
- Gurley's list is the only evaluation scorecard in the batch written for two-sided markets, so it fits the project's shipper × freight-company definition more naturally than single-customer segmentation tools. It can be applied to either the matching opportunity or the software opportunity in a candidate sub-market.
- Three factors carry directly into the project's criteria:
  - High fragmentation on both sides. Measurable with R44, and consistent with R20's "fragmented buyers and ... fragmented sellers".
  - Frequency. Regular shippers tender loads weekly or daily, which is favourable. It is unfavourable where a shipper-broker relationship is "monogamous" (R27 calls brokerage "relationship-driven").
  - Payment flow. A fee taken from savings and billed after the fact is exactly the pattern Gurley says suppliers perceive as a "tax". A fee netted out of a payment the vendor already handles (freight payment, audit recovery, factoring) is perceived as distribution.
- The 7-8/10 rule is a usable pass bar but unvalidated.
- The execution caveat matters: high-scoring freight marketplaces have failed (see the R28 note on Convoy).
