## Strategies or frameworks described

### Evidence-seeking customer conversations ("The Mom Test")
- Origin and intended context: founders talking to customers before and during product building; B2B and B2C; the author says it is used "as a core part of the curriculum at universities like Harvard and UCL, EU startup accelerators like Seedcamp and Microsoft Ventures, and businesses like Shopify and Pact Coffee." *(verified: storefront)*
- Mechanism and steps:
  - Premise (official description, verbatim): "They say you shouldn't ask your mom whether your business is a good idea, because she loves you and will lie to you. This is technically true, but it misses the point. You shouldn't ask anyone if your business is a good idea. It's a bad question and everyone will lie to you at least a little. As a matter of fact, it's not their responsibility to tell you the truth. It's your responsibility to find it". *(verified: Goodreads description)*
  - Scope (storefront, verbatim): "How to avoid biased feedback? How to write an email that makes people want to talk to you? How to figure out whether someone is really going to buy? It's all in here." and "Practical, not academic. The world doesn't need another framework or theory." *(verified)*
  - The three rules (third-party summaries, unverified): "1) Talk about their life instead of your idea, 2) Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future, 3) Talk less and listen more." *(secondary search summary, unverified; consistent with reader knowledge of the book)*
  - Customer slicing (third-party summaries, unverified): "Good customer segments are a who-where pair. If you don't know where to go to find your customers, keep slicing your segment into smaller pieces until you do." *(secondary search summary, unverified)* study_links.md attributes "customer slicing" to the book; that attribution could not be checked in the primary.
  - *Reader knowledge (unverified):* the book treats compliments as noise and treats commitments (time, reputation, money: a follow-up meeting, an introduction, a pre-order or pilot) as the real evidence of demand.
- Criteria, inputs and data required: access to potential customers; a question list about their past behaviour and current spend; notes.
- Output produced: facts about customer problems, current workarounds and spend, and commitments. No selection output.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: *reader inference:* interviews are qualitative and sample sizes small; the book is a technique for gathering evidence, not a method for choosing a market.
- Rationale and evidence base: people are unreliable reporters of future intentions and politely overstate interest ("everyone will lie to you at least a little"). Evidence: author experience ("I've been an entrepreneur for 10 years, running both bootstrapped and VC-backed companies") plus testimonials from John Mullins (R13-adjacent author of *The New Business Road Test*), Seedcamp and Episode1. *(verified: storefront)* No empirical study.
- Key quotes: as above.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:* this is the interview discipline for the problem-discovery stage (S5, and Q2 in CLAUDE.md). It matters most because the project's fee must come out of measurable savings. The questions that produce usable evidence ask what happened. For example: "what did your last claim, detention charge or rejected load cost you, and how did you find out?", or "who handles freight invoices and how long does it take each week?" Asking "would you pay for software that cuts shipping costs?" produces flattering noise. Commitments (sharing freight invoices or TMS exports, an introduction to the carrier or broker, a pilot) are the evidence that a savings baseline can be measured. If "customer slicing" holds, the "who-where" pair is also a practical reachability test (S2): "reefer carriers with fewer than 10 trucks who haul from the Salinas Valley and use DAT" is sliced; "trucking companies" is not. The three rules and slicing are verified only through third-party summaries; buy or borrow the book before quoting it.
