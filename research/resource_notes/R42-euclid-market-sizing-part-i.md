- Decision the resource addresses: market sizing (how to size a beachhead and how big is big enough), with a companion rule for scoring expansion potential (stages S3, S4 and S6). Part II is R24.

## Strategies or frameworks described

### 1. Definitions: SOM, SAM and TAM, and "bottoms-up is best"
- Origin and intended context: vertical software and AI investors writing for early-stage founders who face "TAM skepticism". B2B, venture-backed. *(verified)*
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim):
  - "A good market sizing analysis should illustrate the revenue run-rate a business could eventually reach at a 100% penetration rate of a particular ICP." The analysis runs "on a spectrum from the inception state (first product, current ARPA, beachhead ICP) to a theoretical at-scale state, usually 5-10 years down the road".
  - "Start with a Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) that the business can both service with its products and obtain from a sales perspective out of the gate. We also think of this as the 'beachhead' market." Loyal example: "number of individual-owned senior dogs in the US * percentage seeing obtainable veterinary practices * mean gross spend per owner per dog per year on an existing important pet health vaccine".
  - SAM "assumes a slightly broadened product set and distribution capable of reaching newer, usually larger ICPs." TAM represents "all revenue in the startup's zone of value."
  - Definitions to avoid: "enterprise value of players in the space, ROI users receive from the product, or some broad assumption on percentage capture of total revenue in the space. All of these are predicated on outside assumptions and disconnected from our focus: the startup's ability to distribute and monetize." Footnote 7: "ROI = Return on Investment. In this context, we are using it to mean dollars saved or generated by customers from using the product."
  - "The closer you can tie your market sizing to credible customer needs (e.g. the verifiable size of the problem, with proof of the urgency), the better. As Pear VC explains well in their Market Sizing Guide, where numbers are available, bottoms-up is best (e.g., number of customers in ICP * ARPA)."
  - Doug Leone's test: "What % of the Fortune 2000 will ultimately buy this product… and how much will they be worth to you on average?"
- Criteria, inputs and data required: the count of customers in the ICP, the ARPA, and the obtainable share given distribution.
- Output produced: a bottom-up SOM, then SAM and TAM.
- Rationale and evidence base:
  - Top-down figures, and figures derived from customer ROI, rest on "outside assumptions" and are "disconnected from ... the startup's ability to distribute and monetize".
  - Backed by author authority and a citation of Pear VC.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: "These are such subjective terms that most VCs don't even agree on their definitions."
- Key quotes: see above.

### 2. "Do Big Companies Need Big TAMs?" (an empirical analysis of about 100 public software companies)
- Mechanism and steps: "After pulling ACV, ARR, and EV (Enterprise Value) data for ~100 public horizontal and vertical software companies, we layered on an average of market size estimates we could find from filings / research, and finally, analyzed any systematic relationship between TAM and company size."
- Findings *(verified)*:
  - "the lower bound for TAM on a public software business seems to be about $5B".
  - "93% of TAMs are <$100B, 78% are <$50B, and >20% are $10B or lower".
  - "vertical software companies appear no more TAM-constrained than horizontal peers: mean market size estimates are just ~10% larger for horizontals".
  - "Only six companies in our dataset have ARR greater than 15% of their total stated TAM".
  - "The businesses in our dataset with an average TAM estimate of $10B or less have a mean ARR of ~$480M".
  - "market size in the mid-single digit billions is sufficient to support a venture-scale outcome".
  - Euclid's own rule: "we like to see a potential path to a $15B TAM / $100m ARR—our beachhead SAM, however, could be as low as 10% of those figures." Hence "the floor for a healthy beachhead SOM—the universe a founder can engage from Day 1—likely lands in the vicinity of a few hundred million."
  - TAM does not predict EV once ARR is significant: "the correlation between the two is weak and statistically insignificant by most standards."
- Output produced: size floors (venture-scale) and three conclusions. "Many VCs overestimate the importance of massive TAMs early on and underweight how dynamic they are as a company grows." "At scale, vertical software companies are no more constrained by TAM than horizontal peers". "The ability to grow TAM is more important than TAM itself."
- Rationale and evidence base:
  - Quantitative, small n (about 100), post-IPO companies only.
  - The authors' own caveat: "Between the noise of our TAM assumptions, the small sample size, and the challenges with R-squared as a measure of correlation, we can't hold this up as a definitive scientific finding."
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques:
  - *Reader inference:* survivorship bias (only public companies).
  - The floors assume a venture-scale ambition the project has not adopted.
  - Part II (R24) gives "$100M for a beachhead" where Part I gives "a few hundred million", so the floor itself is loose.
- Key quotes: see above.

### 3. The TAM Uncertainty Principle, and "A Framework for TAM Expansion"
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim):
  - "It's not the TAM you start with; it's the TAM you exit with." (Ed Sim)
  - "the more resolution you demand from TAM at early stages, the more likely it is that a miscalculation obfuscates true growth potential".
  - Three cases:
    - Shopify: VCs saw 40-50k stores as "a $100M market at best"; management estimates of its TAM grew "from $46B in 2015 to $153B in 2020 to nearly $1T today".
    - Uber: network effects allowed a share far above the usual 10% cap, "today, Uber has around 75% of the ride-share market".
    - Toast: began as a check-splitting app and moved to the POS "control point".
  - "As vertical software investors, we at Euclid aim for high resolution on beachhead—that SOM or initial customer set a founder is attacking from Day 1. At inception stages, however, pinning a number on the ultimate TAM is much less important than understanding a founder's vision and capacity for expansion over time."
  - "founders should spend an order of magnitude more time thinking through their TAM expansion strategy than on numerical market sizing ... there must be a credible story around the initial wedge as a driver of adoption and its relevance as a control point to enable downstream expansion opportunities."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: a high-resolution beachhead count; a low-resolution but credible expansion story (wedge, control point, network effects, latent demand).
- Output produced: a two-part sizing standard. Precise for the beachhead; qualitative for the expansion path. The expansion cube is detailed in R24.
- Rationale and evidence base: case studies and quotations from investors (Valentine, Sim, Gurley, Rimer: "We really view TAM as noise").
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: normative. The cases are survivors.
- Key quotes: see above.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:*
- R42 gives the project two operating rules:
  - Size each candidate's beachhead bottom-up (number of target shippers or freight companies × plausible annual fee) at high resolution.
  - Score the expansion path rather than chasing a precise TAM.
- It also creates a direct tension with the project's fee rule. Euclid says not to size markets from "ROI users receive from the product", meaning customer savings. The reconciliation is to use the savings pool only as the ceiling on ARPA: fee = capture share × savings actually realised. Market size is then customer count × that ARPA, never total savings.
- The size floors (SOM of a few hundred million; $15B TAM / $100M ARR) are venture-scale. They should be a tunable parameter set by the project's own ambition, not adopted by default.
