## Strategies or frameworks described

### 1. Five-model pricing taxonomy (report p. 6, "We asked about five different flavors of pricing")
- Origin and intended context: a B2B software vendor survey. Respondents: "A typical respondent was between $1-20M ARR, headquartered in the US and selling a product that's a hybrid of SaaS and AI." *(verified)*
- Mechanism (verbatim definitions):
  - "Outcome or success-based pricing: Customers pay based on the results achieved, such as cost savings or revenue generated. It's performance-driven and shifts risk to the vendor."
  - Hybrid: "Combines two or more pricing models—like a base subscription plus usage or performance fees ... Another example would be to charge a subscription fee with an outcome bonus."
  - The other three are flat-fee/subscription, seat-based and usage-based.
- Output produced: shared vocabulary for fee design.
- Rationale and evidence base: definitional.
- Key quotes: as above.

### 2. Adoption data on pricing models (report pp. 4, 13, 20; newsletter Takeaways 1 and 4)
- Mechanism and steps: a survey "run from April to May 2025" of 240 companies.
  - ARR mix: 26% <$1M, 22% $1-5M, 15% $5-20M, 37% >$20M.
  - Geography: 57% US, 24% Europe or Israel, 5% UK, 14% elsewhere.
  - Product: 50% mostly SaaS, 14% AI-native, 36% hybrid.
  - Recruitment was through the author's newsletter: "This wouldn't be possible without the help of Growth Unhinged readers".
- Findings *(verified from the text layer)*:
  - Primary pricing model, Q2 2024 → Q2 2025 (p. 20):

    | Model | Q2 2024 | Q2 2025 |
    |---|---|---|
    | Flat-fee | 29% | 22% |
    | Seat-based | 21% | 15% |
    | Usage-based | 20% | 17% |
    | Outcome-based | 3% | 5% |
    | Hybrid | 27% | 41% |

  - Expected by Q2 2028: outcome-based 25%, hybrid 49%, usage-based 20%. Flat-fee and seat-based each read as 3% (my reading of the chart text layer).
  - "5% of survey participants said their primary pricing model is outcome-based right now. However, 25% said they expect it to be outcome-based by 2028." (newsletter)
  - "AI-native companies were 4x more likely to say they have outcome-based pricing (OBP)." (p. 4)
  - Factors most important when pricing AI capabilities (p. 13; bar labels in left-to-right order, my reading):

    | Factor | Share |
    |---|---|
    | Internal costs and margins | 56% |
    | Competitor pricing | 47% |
    | Revenue uplift for the customer | 44% |
    | Productivity gains | 38% |
    | Labor savings | 25% |
    | Vendor displacement | 7% |

  - "29% of companies in the survey reported a gross margin under 60%" (p. 13).
- Output produced: base rates for how common savings-linked pricing is.
- Rationale and evidence base: a self-selected survey, n = 240. There is no weighting, no response rate, and no industry breakdown (freight is not reported separately).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques:
  - *Reader inference:* respondents are software vendors, not freight buyers, so the data describe what vendors do, not what freight customers accept.
  - Expectations for 2028 are intentions, not behaviour.
- Key quotes: "Outcome-based pricing is seen as the 'holy grail'. It's still out of reach for 95% of the market." (newsletter heading, Takeaway 4)

### 3. Outcome-based pricing examples and the work-based versus success-based split (report pp. 21-25; newsletter Takeaway 4)
- Mechanism (verbatim):
  - Units shown on p. 21: "Per successful resolution", "25% per recovered chargeback", "Per demand package or medical chronology", "Per completed task", "Only pay for revenue recovered above a baseline", "Per legal deliverable", "Per automated resolution", "35% success fee".
  - Newsletter: "Some of these outcome-based models could be better understood as work-based pricing (see: EvenUp, Casemark). Others are truly success-based pricing where they take a cut of the upside (see: Chargeflow, Flycode and AirHelp). AirHelp, for example, charges a 35% success fee when it wins compensation for delayed or cancelled flights."
  - Customer-service timeline (p. 24): Intercom (Mar 2023), Zendesk (Aug 2024), Kustomer (Oct 2024), Decagon and Sierra (Dec 2024); "In customer service, outcome-oriented pricing models went from unusual to the market standard." Salesforce (p. 25) now positions its AI pricing as "outcome-based".
  - Intercom co-founder Des Traynor (p. 23): "The thesis of Fin is that we're doing the work for you… The biggest challenge was that we had to make sure a customer knew what a resolution was."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: a countable unit of outcome and a baseline, as in "above a baseline".
- Output produced: templates for fee units.
- Rationale and evidence base: case examples, some of them vendor-reported.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: *Reader inference:* the success-based examples with explicit percentages (35% success fee, 25% of recovered chargebacks) are recovery businesses. Each outcome is a discrete, attributable dollar amount, the closest analogue to a freight overcharge or claims recovery.

### 4. Outcome pricing as a moat (report p. 26, "Illustrative example of outcome-based pricing dynamics")
- Mechanism (verbatim table values):

  | | Category leader | Emerging startup |
  |---|---|---|
  | Success rate | 80% | 60% |
  | Cost of service | $0.20 per task | $0.30 per task |
  | Price | $1 per success | $1 per success |
  | Customer value | $4 ROI per success | $4 ROI per success |
  | Revenue | $0.80 per task | $0.60 per task |
  | Gross margin | 75% | 50% |
  | Customer ROI | $2.40 per task | $1.80 per task |

  Commentary: "the category leader could charge 60% more and still match the emerging startup's customer ROI"; "the emerging startup would need to charge $0 per success to match the ROI – but their break-even price is $0.50 per success"; "These dynamics create a moat for the category leader."
- Rationale and evidence base: illustrative arithmetic, not data.
- *Reader inference:* under outcome pricing the vendor's success rate and delivery cost set the margin. A new entrant with a lower success rate is squeezed, so the entry segment should be one where our success rate can be high early.

### 5. The CAMP test (report p. 27, "The elephant in the room: CAMP."; newsletter Takeaway 4)
- Origin and intended context: the author's own framework, introduced in the newsletter as "a big elephant in the room, though, which I'm calling the CAMP framework. To pull off outcome-based pricing, businesses need outcome consistency, attribution, measurability and predictability (CAMP)".
- Mechanism (verbatim, p. 27):
  - "Consistency. Do all customers value the same outcomes? Or do outcomes need to be customized, leading to a proliferation of bespoke contracts?"
  - "Attribution. Can you convince customers to give your product credit for delivering the outcome? Or do they believe they drove the outcome, with a small assist from you?"
  - "Measurability. Can you measure and report on the outcomes in real-time? Or do you require customer reporting, A/B testing and/or a proof of concept?"
  - "Predictability. Can you predict the outcomes your product will achieve with some level of accuracy? Or do outcomes vary significantly from customer to customer?"
- Criteria, inputs and data required: four yes/no or graded judgements per candidate outcome.
- Output produced: a feasibility verdict for outcome pricing.
- Rationale and evidence base:
  - Each question names the failure it prevents: bespoke contracts, disputes over credit, reliance on customer reporting or proofs of concept, and revenue that swings from customer to customer.
  - It is backed by author authority (a former OpenView pricing lead, per the site; not re-verified) and the case examples. It has not been tested empirically.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: *Reader inference:* CAMP is framed for AI agents doing discrete tasks. For shipping-cost savings, "Predictability" is hit by freight-rate cycles. "Attribution" is hit whenever the market moves the rate, not the software.
- Key quotes: see the definitions.

### 6. Hybrid structures, the fallbacks when pure outcome pricing fails (newsletter Takeaway 3)
- Mechanism (verbatim, selected):
  - "PAYG with a cap. This model offers buyers peace of mind by capping their potential usage/spend. It's increasingly seen with outcome-based models where outcomes are unknown in advance."
  - "Platform fee plus success bonus. In this model, pricing is communicated as a more traditional subscription fee. If the customer gets a better ROI than expected, they pay a bonus or commission on top."
  - Also listed: usage-based tiers, platform fee plus usage, three-part tariff, adaptive flat rate.
- Rationale and evidence base: practitioner observation. Reasons given for hybrid: "Minimal disruption", "Upsell path", "Margins", "Relatively predictable".
- Related evidence: "three-in-four software companies made a change to their pricing last year". Among those that changed, "the median revenue growth uplift was 10%" (p. 29).
- Author's outlook: "I'm still bullish about usage-based and hybrid pricing models, but increasingly I see them as a stepping stone toward work-based and outcome-based pricing."

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:*
- R25 supplies the fee-feasibility gate the project needs. CAMP can be a knock-out and scoring row for every candidate sub-market: which outcome would the fee be tied to, and is it consistent, attributable, measurable in real time and predictable?
- Freight savings differ sharply on CAMP (my inference, to be verified in stage S5):
  - Discrete recoveries (freight-invoice overcharges, duplicate bills, accessorial disputes, detention billing captured, demurrage avoided) resemble the chargeback-recovery and "above a baseline" examples. They score high on attribution and measurability.
  - "Rate savings" against a market benchmark score low on predictability and attribution, because freight rates swing (R34: reefer produce rates fell from $3.62 to $2.23 per mile between 2022 Q1 and 2025 Q4).
- The survey's 5% adoption figure says pure outcome pricing is rare. The realistic default is "platform fee plus success bonus" or capped outcome pricing.
- The moat arithmetic warns that an entrant must pick outcomes it can deliver at a high success rate.
- The sample is software vendors, so none of this shows that freight customers accept savings-share fees. R33 shows gainshares already exist in managed transportation.
