## Strategies or frameworks described

### Marketing definition of a market and its "Key Implications" (sections 2, 2.1, 2.2)
- Origin and intended context: textbook framing for marketing students; contrasts economics with marketing. The author flags the terminology problem up front: "Unfortunately, the terminology used in this area is somewhat confusing across different textbooks. Between them they sometimes use the terms 'market', 'sub-market', 'market segment' and 'product-market' interchangeably."
- Mechanism and steps: two economics definitions are quoted, then the broader one is chosen: "It is usually understood as an organized process by which buyers and sellers exchange goods and services for money." (Chin & Guan, 1996) and "... we use the term (market) to refer to exchanges between buyers and sellers who communicate with each other about the quality and quantity of the product, what buyers are willing and able to pay for a product, and what the sellers must receive in order to produce or sell a product." (Mukherjee, 2005). "This second definition also includes the elements of communication (which is marketing promotion), price and profit incentive. Therefore, this second definition is more appropriate for us in the study of marketing." Marketers "tend to focus on: The exchange taking place between buyers and sellers, Consumer needs: The set of needs, wants, and demands that spark these exchanges, Industry structure: The interplay of competing firms and the range of choices available." (2.1)
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none stated.
- Output produced: a market boundary. Its consequences (2.2, verbatim list): "1. Scope of Competition: A well-defined market shows which firms, channels, and consumer needs are relevant. 2. Strategic Boundaries: Deciding that you operate in 'ready-to-eat food' rather than 'frozen pizza' alone guides how you perceive opportunities and adjacent product lines. 3. Resource Allocation: A broad or narrow definition affects where you focus R&D, marketing spend, and operational efforts."
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (reader inference) the definition is two-sided by construction (buyers, sellers, and the industry structure between them), which is the closest thing on the seed site to the project's joint shipper+carrier framing; but the page never uses that two-sidedness when it enumerates sub-markets, which it does by product category.
- Key quotes: "Examples of Large Markets: Banking Market ... Education Market ... Ready-to-Eat Food Market: Includes all on-the-go meal solutions (frozen dinners, packaged snacks, quick-serve meals, meal kits, etc.)."

### Sub-market as a "Market Within a Market": identification checklist and definition (sections 3.1-3.3)
- Origin and intended context: marketing teaching; the author borrows the concept's pedigree from US antitrust law ("The idea of sub-markets gained prominence in U.S. legal cases like the Brown Shoe Co. v. U.S. (1962)").
- Mechanism and steps: a slice of a market is a sub-market when it has "some unique operational characteristics" (3.1, verbatim list): "Unique product features or attributes, Distinct customer groupings or usage contexts, Specialized competitors who may not compete in the broader market, Different price structures or cost factors, Tailored marketing or distribution methods." The legal indicia (3.2): "Courts described a sub-market as a 'relevant market within a relevant market,' highlighting that sub-markets have unique: Product features, Distinct customer bases, Pricing patterns, Retail or distribution approaches." The author's working definition (3.2): "A smaller and more defined sector of an overall market, which has a number of differing marketing and structural features, which may include distinct distribution channels, price elasticity, competitive sets, and effective promotional methods."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: evidence on product features, customer groupings and usage contexts, competitor sets, price structures/cost factors/price elasticity, channels and promotion. No data sources are named; the examples are asserted.
- Output produced: a list of sub-markets, each with "its own competitive set and marketing strategy" ("While a multinational bank might operate in all these areas, a niche online lender might focus only on home loans or student refinancing. Each sub-market thus fosters its own competitive set and marketing strategy.").
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (stated) "From a marketer's perspective, these sub-markets require tailored strategies, because what works in one sub-market may be ineffective in another." (reader inference) The five-item checklist is the most usable thing on the seed site: it is a TEST for whether a candidate slice is a real sub-market (distinctness on several structural features at once), not a way to generate candidates or rank them. Two of the five items are demand-side (customer groupings/usage contexts; price elasticity) and three are supply-side, so the test is inherently two-sided. (reader inference, unverified against the court opinion) the Brown Shoe "practical indicia" are a rigorous primary source for this checklist and are not on the study list.
- Key quotes: "A sub-market is essentially a smaller or more specialized sector within a larger market." (3.1) "Note: the 'The Brown Shoe case' [Brown Shoe Co. v. U.S. 1962], where a sub-market was referred to as 'a relevant market within a relevant market'. As part of their deliberations, they indicated that sub-markets were likely to have products with unique characteristics, distinct customers, distinct pricing, and even specialized retailers." (3.2)

### Product-market: "Defining 'Where to Compete?'", the Product/Market Grid, and "Variation in Definition" (sections 4.1-4.3)
- Origin and intended context: incumbent-firm strategy; the examples are a technology firm choosing smart-home devices and a bank choosing credit cards plus online savings.
- Mechanism and steps: (4.1) "A product-market is typically defined when a firm decides which products it will offer in which markets or sub-markets. You might hear the phrase 'where to compete?'—this is about mapping the intersection of product lines and specific market segments or sub-markets." (4.2, verbatim) "Firms often use a product/market grid to visualize their chosen territory: Rows: The product categories or specific lines (e.g., standard credit cards, premium credit cards, small-business loans, etc.), Columns: The markets or sub-markets they plan to operate in (e.g., consumer banking, small-business banking, wealth clientele). This grid helps teams see gaps (unserved markets) and ensure alignment of resources. One firm's product-market definition might be narrower or broader based on its strategic goals." (4.3, verbatim) "Each firm has its own perspective on a product-market, influenced by: Core competencies: Technology expertise, brand strength, distribution networks. Strategic vision: Whether they want to target premium or mass-market customers. Competitive landscape: Some product-market combos might already be dominated by entrenched rivals, prompting the firm to focus on a different slice."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: a list of the firm's product lines (rows) and a list of markets/sub-markets (columns); knowledge of which cells are served and by whom. Selection factors: core competencies, strategic vision, competitive landscape (entrenched rivals).
- Output produced: a grid of product x (sub-)market cells; the firm's chosen cells; visible "gaps (unserved markets)".
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (reader inference) the grid is the one enumeration device on the seed site that is naturally two-sided: for freight, rows can be carrier service types or freight-company types and columns shipper groups, and every cell is a candidate two-sided sub-market. The "gaps" reading is an opportunity heuristic, but the page says nothing about how to score cells; "dominated by entrenched rivals" is the only exclusion criterion, and it is unquantified. The grid presumes the firm already has product lines, which a pre-product startup does not.
- Key quotes: "A banking institution might define its 'product-market' scope as offering credit cards and online savings, but ignoring wealth management or home loans." (4.1)

### "Common Confusions and How to Avoid Them" (sections 5.1-5.3) and "Importance of Distinguishing These Concepts" (section 6)
- Origin and intended context: teaching aid.
- Mechanism and steps: (5.1, verbatim) "1. Market: The broad collection of buyers and sellers. 2. Sub-Market: A defined slice of that larger market based on products or specialized demand. 3. Market Segment: Groups of consumers within a (sub-)market who share common needs or characteristics. Tip: Ensure you keep sub-markets (which are product-based categories) distinct from market segments (which are consumer-based groups). Failing to do so often leads to product-oriented thinking instead of consumer-centered segmentation." (5.2) "a product-market specifically identifies both what product or service you are offering and where you're offering it. In contrast, a sub-market classification (e.g., 'credit cards') doesn't specify which particular credit card variants or consumer sets you're targeting within that domain." (5.3 worked example) "We are in the credit card sub-market and have three product-markets: (1) youth starter credit cards, (2) frequent traveler rewards cards, and (3) small-business expense cards."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none.
- Output produced: correct labelling of each level. Section 6 lists four payoffs (verbatim headings): "Strategic Clarity", "Competitive Understanding: Each sub-market might have specialized players. Understanding this helps identify who you're truly competing with.", "Efficient Resource Allocation", "Effective Segmentation: By not mixing sub-markets with consumer segments, you can identify real consumer-based segments within each product or sub-market category."
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (stated) sub-markets are "based on products or specialized demand" (5.1), a slightly wider phrasing than R01's "product-driven categories"; "specialized demand" admits a demand-side sub-market. (reader inference) nothing here ranks anything.
- Key quotes: as above.

### "Step-by-Step: How to Classify a Market, Sub-Market, and Product-Market" (section 8; five steps)
- Origin and intended context: the page's only procedure; written for a firm that already knows its offering ("where do you plan to offer your product(s)?").
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim, the author's ordering):
  "1. Identify the Broad Market: Ask, 'In which general arena of needs/demands do we operate?' Example: 'We're in the personal transportation market.'
  2. Break Out Sub-Markets: List the product or service categories typically recognized within that arena. E.g., 'Ride-sharing,' 'Automotive sales,' 'Bike sharing,' 'Public transport passes,' etc.
  3. Define Your Product-Market Scope: Among those sub-markets, where do you plan to offer your product(s)? Are you focusing on 'electric bike sharing in major cities' or 'budget-friendly secondhand car sales in suburban areas'?
  4. Drill Down to Consumer Segments: Identify the consumer groups within the chosen sub-market who share similar purchase motivations, usage behaviors, or psychographic traits.
  5. Check for Confusion: If you find that your 'segments' are just different product categories, you may be incorrectly mixing sub-markets with market segments."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: step 2 relies on "product or service categories typically recognized within that arena", i.e. industry convention, not data; step 3 gives no criterion for the choice; step 4 uses purchase motivations, usage behaviours, psychographics.
- Output produced: a labelled hierarchy (market, sub-markets, chosen product-market scope, segments) plus a consistency check.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (reader inference) step 2 is "list what the industry already calls things", which will reproduce the industry's supply-side taxonomy (for freight: mode and service type); step 3 is the selection step and is entirely unspecified; the procedure is classification, not choice. The transport example (ride-sharing, bike sharing, public transport passes) is passenger transport, not freight.
- Key quotes: as above.

- Large markets: banking, education, ready-to-eat food (2.1).
- Sub-markets (3.3): Banking: Credit Cards ("Distinct players like American Express, specialized distribution via direct mail, partnerships with department stores"), Home Loans ("large financial institutions, or niche mortgage brokers, rely on real estate agents and in-person consultations"), Online Savings ("digital channels, often appealing to tech-savvy or rate-sensitive customers"). Automotive: Electric Vehicles ("Specialized manufacturers, unique charging infrastructure, early adopters"), Luxury Sedans, Commercial Trucks ("B2B client base, different marketing channels and service networks"). Ready-to-Eat Food: Frozen Meals (Lean Cuisine, Stouffer's), Meal Kits ("Subscription-based services, direct-to-consumer distribution"), Convenience Store Snacks.

- Additional examples (7.1-7.2): Software: overall market enterprise software; sub-markets CRM, ERP, Project Management Tools; product-markets "A CRM platform tailored for small businesses, An ERP solution for large manufacturers, A subscription-based project management tool for agile tech startups." Retail: sub-markets clothing, electronics, home furnishings; product-markets budget family clothing, premium brand electronics, trendy urban home decor.
- Step-by-step example (8): personal transportation market.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
(Judgement.) R02 is the most useful of the three seed pages for the project's framing because two of its devices are two-sided by construction: the five-item "market within a market" checklist (two demand-side items, three supply-side) works as a knock-out test for whether a candidate freight slice is a genuine sub-market rather than a label, and the product/market grid, with freight-company service types as rows and shipper groups as columns, is a concrete way to enumerate candidate two-sided sub-markets and to read empty or thinly-served cells as opportunity signals. What it lacks is any ranking: the three "variation in definition" factors (core competencies, strategic vision, entrenched rivals) are the only selection criteria and are neither weighted nor measurable, and its five-step procedure classifies rather than chooses (step 3, the choice, is a question with no method). Its step 2, "list the categories typically recognized within that arena", is a warning as much as an instruction: it will reproduce the freight industry's own mode/service taxonomy unless the shipper axis is added deliberately. The Brown Shoe pedigree of the checklist suggests a rigorous primary source (antitrust sub-market indicia) that the study list does not include.
