## Strategies or frameworks described

### "Reasons for Defining Markets" (section 1; six reasons, restated in the closing table)
- Origin and intended context: general management/marketing teaching; applies to "any organization—large or small, new or established" (section 7).
- Mechanism and steps: six reasons, each introduced by a question (verbatim headings and questions): 1.1 Business Definition, "What business are we really in?"; 1.2 Strategy Development, "Where to compete and how?"; 1.3 Search for Opportunities, "Are there hidden needs we can serve?"; 1.4 Defining Competitors, "Who are we really up against?"; 1.5 Focusing Resources, "How do we keep our eyes on the prize?"; 1.6 Market Segmentation, "It's the first step in STP (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning)."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none.
- Output produced: a statement of "this is our market" that "shap[es] where they will compete and how they will view opportunities and threats."
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (stated) "No firm can segment a market without first defining it." (1.6) (reader inference) 1.3 is the only reason that bears on problem discovery: "By mapping out which market segments a firm aims to serve, it may reveal: Overlooked niches, such as a new demographic subset not targeted by current offerings. Adjacent product categories or service lines where consumer needs intersect with the firm's capabilities." and "Explicit market boundaries keep the firm alert to changes and emerging sub-markets".
- Key quotes (closing table, verbatim): "Business definition | Most businesses prefer to have a clear understanding of what business they are in. Defining the scope of their markets provides a framework for their operations"; "Strategy development | ... Clear market descriptions and boundaries provides significant guidance in their strategy and planning process"; "Search for opportunities | Clearly mapping out the range of markets they are pursuing may help identify opportunities and unmet needs"; "Define competitors | Defining markets will enable the firm to more effectively identify its direct and indirect competitors"; "Focus resources | Clear market definitions will ensure that management and other resources are continually focused on the identified market/s, rather than considering a range of other potential opportunities"; "Market segmentation | Organizations need to define markets as the first step in their market segmentation process". Also 1.4: "If a fast-food chain defines its market as strictly 'burgers,' it might only compare itself to other burger chains. If instead it sees itself in the broader 'quick-service dining' market, it will consider all chains offering fast, convenient meals (like pizza or Mexican). Some quick-service restaurants even define their market as 'affordable eating-out options,' thus encompassing grocery deli counters or meal-prep services as indirect rivals."

### "Main Approaches to Defining a Market" (section 2; four approaches)
- Origin and intended context: "The approach often depends on the industry, the data available, and the firm's strategic objectives." Approach 2.4 is explicitly for "smaller, local businesses" and "business-to-business (B2B) contexts or local service industries".
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim, the author's ordering):
  2.1 Industry Classification: "Many firms use government or industry data—like the North American Industry Classification System (NAICS)—to identify their market. NAICS codes can range from broad categories (e.g., Manufacturing) to very specific sectors (e.g., Computer Peripheral Equipment Manufacturing). Benefits of using industry classifications: Consistent across government and economic reports, aiding in benchmarking against official statistics. Clarity in identifying peers and competitor groupings. Easier to track macro-level trends (e.g., growth rate, total market size, export figures). However, it can also be limiting if it overlooks overlaps between industries or if a firm's product cuts across multiple classifications."
  2.2 Product Category: "define markets by product type or category, such as: Cars, Food retail, Publishing, Professional services. Defining the market through the lens of similar products helps highlight substitutes. A shampoo brand that sees itself in the 'hair care' market might track not only other shampoo brands but also scalp treatments or hair growth supplements. One downside is that consumer needs or use-cases might span categories (like hair care merging with skincare for holistic wellness)."
  2.3 Geographic Scope: "By country (e.g., 'the US consumer electronics market'), By region (e.g., 'the Latin American automotive market'), Local area or city boundaries (e.g., 'the Chicago fast-casual dining market'). Clear geographic boundaries help in planning logistics, distribution, and cultural adaptation of marketing strategies."
  2.4 Yellow Pages / Business Directories: "For smaller, local businesses, referencing something like the Yellow Pages or specific business directories can be a pragmatic approach to see who else operates in the same space. It is particularly common in business-to-business (B2B) contexts or local service industries: Landscaping, Accountancy, Specialty retail niches. This approach is straightforward but can become narrow if a firm looks for new audiences or if technology disrupts the existing directory categories."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: NAICS (or equivalent) codes and the official statistics keyed to them; product-category conventions; geographic units; business directories.
- Output produced: a bounded market expressed as an industry code, a product category, a geography, or a directory category (or a combination).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (stated) NAICS "can also be limiting if it overlooks overlaps between industries or if a firm's product cuts across multiple classifications"; product category misses cross-category needs; directories "can become narrow". (reader inference) For a two-sided freight sub-market the NAICS caveat is the operative one: shippers and freight companies sit in different NAICS sectors (agriculture and manufacturing on one side; transportation and warehousing, incl. trucking and freight arrangement, on the other), so a joint sub-market is exactly a "product [that] cuts across multiple classifications" and needs two codes, one per side. The page does not say this; it is the reader's extension.
- Key quotes: "Firms can define markets in various ways. The approach often depends on the industry, the data available, and the firm's strategic objectives."

### "Why Defining Product-Market Scope Matters" and alignment with STP (section 3)
- Origin and intended context: incumbent strategy; examples are Apple and Netflix.
- Mechanism and steps: (3.1) the definition is "Here is our playground—where we compete now and potentially might expand or innovate in the future" and "a market definition often becomes a compass for spotting future adjacency moves or product expansions." (3.2) "Firms constantly face the question: Where should they deploy resources? Big choices like entering emerging markets (e.g., India or Brazil), launching new product lines, or competing for specific consumer demographics hinge on how the firm conceptualizes its overall market." (3.3, verbatim) "A firm deciding it is in the 'automotive market' might segment based on vehicle type or consumer lifestyle. Another brand that sees itself in the 'mobility solutions market' might segment differently—by usage context (car-sharing, short-distance electric scooters, etc.). In the STP framework: 1. Segmentation: Break the defined market into subgroups. 2. Targeting: Choose which subgroups to serve. 3. Positioning: Determine how to differentiate and communicate your offering to those groups. Hence, a fuzzy or overly broad market definition complicates or even undermines segmentation and subsequent strategy decisions."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none.
- Output produced: the segmentation basis follows from the definition (vehicle type vs usage context).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (reader inference) the useful point is that the CHOICE OF DEFINITION FIXES THE SEGMENTATION AXIS: "automotive" leads to product-type segments, "mobility solutions" to usage-context segments. For freight this is the argument that defining the market from the shipper's job (moving product X from A to B under constraints) rather than from carrier mode yields different, and possibly more problem-revealing, sub-markets.
- Key quotes: "If Apple had stuck to 'personal computers' only, would it have branched into smartphones, wearables, or streaming services? Probably not. If Netflix had defined its market purely as 'DVD rentals,' it wouldn't have pivoted into streaming and later original content." (3.2)

### "What Happens If a Market Is Poorly Defined?" (section 4)
- Origin and intended context: cautionary section.
- Mechanism and steps: three failure modes (verbatim headings): 4.1 "Confusion in Operations and Strategy"; 4.2 "Missed Opportunities": "If a firm is overly narrow—like considering only 'smartphones' when they could also play in 'personal devices'—they may fail to notice adjacent growth areas ... Conversely, if they are overly broad ('consumer technology' without specifying sub-domains), they risk lacking a specialized identity and losing focus."; 4.3 "Misidentification of Competitors": "a taxi firm ignoring rideshare apps might see them as 'tech platforms' instead of direct competition in the broader 'urban mobility' space. The result is strategic blind spots and slower reaction to emerging threats."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none.
- Output produced: none (diagnostic).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (reader inference) the taxi/rideshare example is the page's closest analogue to a software entrant in a transport market: software platforms are competitors to service providers under a broad definition, and invisible under a narrow one.
- Key quotes: as above.

### "The Interplay Between Market Definition and Opportunity Sizing" (section 5; top-down vs bottom-up)
- Origin and intended context: one paragraph; general.
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim): "Defining a market also involves quantifying it—estimating size, growth, and profit potential. A thorough approach often uses a 'top-down' or 'bottom-up' market sizing method: Top-down: Start with broad industry stats (like total beverage sales in a country) and narrow down to the relevant category. Bottom-up: Aggregate sales from existing distributors, channels, or competitor analysis to estimate total reachable sales. Whichever approach is used, a precise definition of what market you are measuring remains the foundation for accurate sizing. Without it, numbers may be misleading or inconsistent across departments."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: industry statistics (top-down); distributor/channel/competitor sales (bottom-up).
- Output produced: size, growth, profit potential of the defined market.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (reader inference) this is the only place on the three seed pages where a quantitative input to selection is named; it sizes revenue, not the cost or waste pool, whereas the project's fee-out-of-savings rule needs the latter. The sizing quantities to substitute are shipping-and-handling spend and the avoidable share of it per candidate sub-market.
- Key quotes: as above.

### "Balancing Breadth and Depth in Market Definitions" with "Iterative Refinement" (section 6)
- Origin and intended context: general.
- Mechanism and steps: (6.1) "being too restrictive can stifle innovation and future growth. For instance: A cosmetic brand that only sees 'lipstick' as its market might overlook expansions into skincare or fragrances"; (6.2) "a tech company that sees itself as simply in the 'digital market' lumps countless products and consumer needs together, making it hard to prioritize. They might overreach or attempt too many product lines."; (6.3, verbatim) "A firm's chosen market scope may evolve over time: 1. Start with a moderate definition, reflecting current offerings. 2. Review strategic opportunities: Should we expand or pivot? 3. Revise the market definition if brand expansions or changing consumer behaviors justify a broader or different lens."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none stated; "moderate" is undefined.
- Output produced: a revised definition over time.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (stated) step 1 anchors on "current offerings", which a pre-product venture lacks. (reader inference) the breadth/depth tension is the same one the project faces between "all freight" (too broad to prioritise) and a single lane or commodity (too narrow to size); the page offers no rule for where "moderate" lies.
- Key quotes: as above.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
(Judgement.) R03 is a "why" page with one usable "how": the four bounding approaches (industry classification, product category, geography, directories) are a serviceable checklist of axes for enumerating candidate freight sub-markets, and its own NAICS caveat, that a product "cuts across multiple classifications", is the precise reason a two-sided freight sub-market needs a shipper-side code and a carrier-side code rather than one. Its sharpest transferable idea is that the choice of definition fixes the segmentation axis (automotive gives vehicle-type segments; mobility gives usage-context segments), which for this project means defining candidates from the shipper's shipping job rather than from carrier mode if the aim is to surface problems. The sizing paragraph names the right inputs in the wrong currency for the project (revenue, not cost or waste pool), and the iterative-refinement loop is sensible but anchored on "current offerings", which a pre-product venture does not have. Nothing here ranks or selects; it belongs to the market-definition step before Q1's method question, and its grade C is fair.
