## Strategies or frameworks described

### The three conditions that make a set of end users "a market" (certification before choosing)
- Origin and intended context: innovation-driven start-ups, end-user focused; the analogy is military ("choosing a small market segment you can control until your company has sufficient resources to enter other markets"). *(verified)*
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim): "First, ask yourself: Are the markets from your Market Segmentation in Step 1 well-defined enough and small enough to attack successfully and not only conquer but dominate? You will compare each market against the three conditions that define a market ... Same product: The end users will all use the same product. If you have to make slightly different products to appeal to the different end users in your market, it's too broad of a market. Same sales process: The sales process to the end users will be the same, meaning interchangeable language, value proposition, sales channels, etc. If your salespeople have to change tactics from one end user to the next, your markets are not targeted enough! Word of mouth: There is strong word of mouth between end users in this community. If your end users don't talk to each other—if they are separated by large distances, for example—you're going to have a hard time building up sales across the full group of end users. Refine it!" Then: "it's almost always a requirement to further segment your markets." *(verified)*
- Worksheet 02.1 checklist (verbatim): "1. Are all market segments actually market segments in that they meet all of the three criteria for a market (similar product, similar sales process, word of mouth)? 2. Do your market segments identify actual human end users and not just general companies or departments in companies? 3. Are the market segments segmented down into reasonable sizes for your start-up to address (i.e., less than a billion and more than a few hundred thousand)? (More on this in Step 4.) 4. If all three of the above criteria are not met, then continue to iterate on the market segmentation matrix until they are." Item 5 asks for "an intellectually honest attempt (i.e., without biases as much as possible)". *(verified)* The size band gives no unit. *Reader inference:* it is presumably annual revenue potential (TAM, which Step 4 calculates).
- Criteria, inputs and data required: product sameness, sales-process sameness, word-of-mouth links, human end users, size band.
- Output produced: a certified list of candidate markets (or a return to Step 1 to re-segment).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: all qualitative; the size band is a rule of thumb without units. *(reader inference)*
- Rationale and evidence base: each condition carries its own reason in the text: different products mean the market is "too broad"; different sales tactics mean it is "not targeted enough"; without word of mouth "you're going to have a hard time building up sales across the full group". Evidence: author authority and teaching experience; no data.
- Key quotes: page tagline: "Choose a single market segment to pursue; then, keep segmenting until you have a well-deﬁned and homogenous market opportunity that meets the three conditions of a market. Focus is your ally." *(verified)*

### Choosing the beachhead: the same eight criteria, rated in a discussion worksheet, decided by the whole team
- Origin and intended context: same.
- Mechanism and steps:
  - Criteria (page, verbatim): "Is the target customer well-funded? Is the target customer readily accessible to your sales force? Does the target customer have a compelling reason to buy? Can you deliver a whole product? Is there entrenched competition? Can you leverage this market segment to enter others? Is the market consistent with the values, passions, and goals of the team? How quickly can you win this market?" (the page says these are "the same eight key criteria that you used during the Step 1 Market Segmentation"; the full wording and reasons are in R15). *(verified)*
  - Worksheet 02.2 "Beachhead Market Selection (A Framework for Discussion)" (verbatim labels): a grid with one column per candidate ("Market Segment = ____", four columns) and rows "1. Economically attractive / 2. Accessible to your sales force / 3. Strong value proposition / 4. Complete product / 5. Competition / 6. Strategic value / 7. Personal alignment / 8. Estimated time to win / Overall rating / Key factors are most important contributors to the ranking / Ranking / Key deciding factors". Rating scale: "Rating is Very High (best - 1), High, Medium, Low, Show Stopper (worst - 4)". *(verified)* *Reader note:* the scale names five levels but numbers only 1 and 4; the source is internally inconsistent. No weights and no arithmetic are given; the overall rating and ranking are team judgements, and the sheet asks the team to name the "key deciding factors".
  - Process tips (verbatim): "Involve the whole founding team ... You want to finish this step with increased cohesion on your team." "Avoid 'analysis paralysis.' There is almost always more than one path to success. Your goal is to eliminate the paths with the lowest odds of success and choose one path with relatively good odds. Then, take your chosen path and pursue it without thinking about 'what could've been'—burn the boats! Pursue it until it proves not to work or is much less attractive than originally anticipated." "Keep doing primary market research! Part of why you narrow your selections is because primary market research is time-consuming and expensive in terms of opportunity costs, so you want to focus your research on compelling candidates for Beachhead Market." "Use the Beachhead Market Selection worksheet to compile your notes on your potential Beachhead Markets and to rank and make a decision about which will be your beachhead. It may take several iterations, and you might not end up being right with your ultimate selection. But if you later determine your selection will not work, you can always come back ... and restart at this point with more confidence and skill". "Finally, before you move on to the next step, all of your team members must sign the Agreement on the Beachhead Market Selection." *(verified)*
- Criteria, inputs and data required: the eight criteria; PMR notes from Step 1 (R15's 17-row matrix); team deliberation.
- Output produced: one beachhead market, a ranking of the others, the key deciding factors, and a signed team agreement.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: *reader inference:* the method is deliberately unweighted and ordinal. It relies on team judgement disciplined by PMR, not on a formula. "Show Stopper" acts as an embedded knock-out. There is no explicit criterion for how a customer pays or for whether savings are measurable.
- Rationale and evidence base: focus ("Focus is your ally"). Speed over precision ("There is almost always more than one path to success"). PMR cost ("time-consuming and expensive ... so you want to focus your research on compelling candidates"). Team cohesion: "in my experience, failure to address team issues upfront is the number one reason companies fail. The hardest part of a startup is not technology or strategy, it's people." Evidence: author experience and MIT teaching; no data.
- Key quotes: as above.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:* R16 supplies the last narrowing step (S6) and a validity test (S2). The three conditions (same product, same sales process, word of mouth) are a strong test of whether a candidate freight sub-market is one market or several. "Produce shippers" probably fails "same product" across reefer truckload, rail and export containers until it is sliced further. Word of mouth is a real, checkable property in freight: co-ops, commodity associations, broker networks and load-board communities. The worksheet is a ready template. Its ordinal Very High ... Show Stopper scale is suitable when evidence is thin, and its "Key deciding factors" row is a cheap way to record why the choice was made, which the project's CLAUDE.md requires. Adaptations: add fee-from-savings rows ("savings measurable and attributable", "who pays") and note that "Economically attractive" should be read as the savings pool, not the freight spend. "Burn the boats" should be read with R40's evidence for keeping backup options (see synthesis §4).
