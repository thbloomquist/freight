## Strategies or frameworks described

### ADAMS: Accessible, Differentiable, Actionable, Measurable, Substantial
- Origin and intended context: introductory marketing textbook; examples are consumer products (breakfast drink, advertising reach). *(verified)*
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim definitions):
  - "a market segment should be accessible. Can you reach consumers in that segment at an affordable cost, given the strengths and abilities of your marketing department?"
  - "a market segment should be differentiable. In an ideal world, a market segment should be internally homogeneous (i.e., consumers within that segment have similar preferences and characteristics) but externally heterogeneous (i.e., different segments should be quite distinct and different from each other)."
  - "a market segment should be actionable. Is it practical (or profitable) to execute a marketing strategy aimed at that segment? A market segment should be able to respond to a certain marketing strategy and have outcomes—e.g., awareness, interest, or purchase—that can be easily quantified."
  - "a market segment should be measurable. You should be able to accurately estimate the size of the market segment in terms of either sales value or number of customers so that you can decide whether, how, and to what extent you should focus your efforts on that segment."
  - "a market segment should be substantial. It doesn't make sense to waste resources to market the product or service to a group too small to justify the expenditure of resources." *(all verified)*
- Criteria, inputs and data required: reach cost vs capability; within-group homogeneity and between-group distinctness; practicality/profitability of acting; a size estimate (sales value or customer count); minimum size.
- Output produced: pass/fail per test (the section's quiz treats them as diagnostic checks).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: *reader inference:* no thresholds are given (how small is "too small", how accessible is "affordable"), so each test needs a project-specific cut-off. The tests check a segment's usability, not its attractiveness or our ability to win. As study_links.md notes, the section does not cover the attractiveness-evaluation rule.
- Rationale and evidence base: each test is justified by avoiding wasted resources ("doesn't make sense to waste resources", "so that you can decide whether, how, and to what extent you should focus your efforts"). Textbook authority; no evidence cited.
- Key quotes: as above.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:* ADAMS is the cheapest first gate (S2), and each test translates to freight with a project-specific threshold. The freight readings below are reader inference:
- Accessible: can we reach both the shipper group and its freight companies at low cost, e.g. through trade associations, load boards, co-ops or TMS marketplaces?
- Differentiable: is the candidate really distinct from neighbouring candidates? For example, do reefer-produce shippers behave differently from dry-grocery shippers? This overlaps R02's sub-market indicia and R16's "same product / same sales process" conditions.
- Actionable: can a software product change outcomes there, and can those outcomes be measured? This is where the project's "software-improvable" and "measurable savings" rules naturally attach.
- Measurable: can we size the candidate from data? Agent B's datasets (FAF5, CFS, Census concentration tables) make many freight cells measurable.
- Substantial: is the savings pool big enough to carry a fee?
