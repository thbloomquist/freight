### Segment evaluation rule: segment size and growth, segment structural attractiveness, company objectives and resources
- Origin and intended context: Kotler's standard targeting rule (study_links.md: "size and growth, structural attractiveness using the five forces at segment level, fit with company objectives and resources").
- Mechanism and steps: *not verified in the primary.* Web-search summary of secondary restatements (unverified): "Kotler (1990) suggests considering three key factors: segment size and growth, segment structural attractiveness, and company objectives and resources"; "The largest, fastest-growing segments are not always the most attractive ones for every company. Smaller companies may lack the skills and resources needed to serve larger segments or they may find these segments too competitive"; "A segment is less attractive if it already contains many strong and aggressive competitors or if it is easy for new entrants to come into the segment"; "Some attractive segments can be dismissed quickly because they do not mesh with the company's long-run objectives. Or the company may lack the skills and resources needed to succeed". *(secondary search summary, unverified)*
- Criteria, inputs and data required: segment sales, growth rates, expected profitability; competitor strength, entry ease, substitutes, buyer and supplier power (Porter's forces, R43); fit with objectives and resources.
- Output produced: a judgement per segment; no scoring procedure in the restatements.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: *reader inference:* the three factors are the textbook ancestor of the two-axis matrices in R12/R14. Factors 1-2 correspond to attractiveness and factor 3 to fit/strength.
- Rationale and evidence base: textbook authority; the restatements give the rationale that the biggest segments are not the best for small firms.
- Key quotes: none verified.

### Target-market strategies (OpenStax §5.5, secondary)
- Mechanism and steps: four strategies: undifferentiated (mass), differentiated (segmented), concentrated (niche), micromarketing. Concentrated marketing: "the company focuses on just one target market with a single marketing mix ... with the aim of 'owning' it over its competitors and creating strong brand loyalty." "This is a smart strategy for smaller companies or companies with limited resources that might be stretched thin if they attempt to compete in too many market segments. It has the added benefit that R&D and marketing can concentrate on understanding and meeting the needs of one group of customers rather than a diverse base of customers." *(verified in OpenStax §5.5)*
- Rationale and evidence base: textbook authority (the examples are LUSH, Nike, Honda, Stitch Fix).

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:* R09 is the authority behind two rules the process will use: the usable-segment tests (read free in R10) and the three-factor evaluation rule (size and growth, structural attractiveness, objectives and resources). The second rule is the simplest defensible frame for the scoring stage, and it maps directly onto the two axes of R12/R14. It could not be verified in the primary: the chapter is paywalled and the free restatement found was blocked. The process doc should cite it as "per Kotler, as restated by secondary sources and study_links.md" until a library copy is checked. The concentrated-strategy rationale (limited resources; R&D focused on one group's needs) independently supports a single beachhead sub-market for a pre-revenue team.
