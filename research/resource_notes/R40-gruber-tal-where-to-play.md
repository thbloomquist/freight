## Strategies or frameworks described

### The Market Opportunity Navigator: Identify → Evaluate → Prioritize (three worksheets)
- Origin and intended context: start-ups ("find your best starting position") and corporate innovators ("identify growth opportunities beyond your industry boundaries"); technology-push ventures especially. It is positioned as "the front-end of Customer Development" (Blank). *(verified)*
- Mechanism and steps (site, verbatim):
  - "STEP 1 Generate your Market Opportunity Set. Your core abilities or technologies can create different offerings for different types of customers ... A varied set of market opportunities is an asset in and of itself, as it increases your chances of focusing on the most promising option. It also provides the basis for plan-B, if required, and for unlocking new growth opportunities over time. Worksheet 1 will help you to: Understand the general functionalities of your core abilities / Discover new applications and customer segments / Get a wide-lens perspective beyond your industry boundaries."
  - "STEP 2 Evaluate Market Opportunity Attractiveness ... A systematic evaluation process makes sure that you haven't forgotten any key consideration, and allows you to visually map your options and compare them. It provides the basis for team discussions so that you are less susceptible to biases and intuition. Worksheet 2 will help you to: Assess the value creation potential of each market opportunity / Estimate the overall challenge in capturing this value / Map out your opportunities to better grasp their upsides and downsides."
  - "STEP 3 Design your Agile Focus Strategy. Market choice requires commitment which may lock you in. Yet playing in an uncertain world demands flexibility ... By consciously keeping related options open, you can mitigate your risk and avoid a potentially fatal lock-in. ... Worksheet 3 will help you to: Clearly define the primary market opportunity that you will focus on / Design a smart portfolio of backup and growth options to keep open / Say 'no' to all other options." *(all verified)*
  - Blank's summary of the three steps: "Identify a portfolio of market opportunities stemming from your technology or unique abilities / Reveal the most attractive domain(s) by evaluating the potential and challenges of each option / Prioritize market opportunities smartly to set the boundaries for your lean experimentations". *(verified)*
- Criteria, inputs and data required:
  - Attractiveness Map axes: "value creation potential" and "the overall challenge in capturing this value" *(verified)*. Tal and Gruber in Blank's article: "market domains vary in their value creation potential, competitive landscape, regulatory regime and risks associated with launching new products" *(verified)*.
  - **The exact sub-criteria for potential and for challenge sit in the gated Worksheet 2 and were not verified.** *Reader knowledge (unverified; must be checked against the worksheet):* potential is commonly given as compelling value to customers, market size/volume, market growth and economic viability. Challenge is commonly given as implementation obstacles, time to revenue, external funding needs and competitive threat. Map regions are commonly labelled "gold mines", "moonshots", "quick wins" and "questionable". Only the label "gold mine" is verified, from the Flyability case in Blank's article.
- Output produced: a mapped set of options; one primary opportunity, a small portfolio of backup and growth options kept open, and everything else explicitly parked ("placed in storage", "focusing is about saying no more than anything else", Blank). *(verified)*
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: *reader inference:* the method starts from "core abilities or technologies" (technology push), whereas this project starts from a market and a problem hypothesis. Our "core ability" is software/automation capability plus whatever freight access the team has.
- Rationale and evidence base:
  - Stated purposes: "Reduce uncertainty / Make unbiased team decisions / Move forward with confidence" and "Stop playing target market roulette". Tal and Gruber (in Blank): "by not asking 'Where to Play' innovators risk choosing an inferior playground ... this early decision may be difficult to change, or even irreversible: it influences how you develop your technology going forward, raise money, write patents, recruit employees and pick a brand name." *(verified)*
  - Claimed evidence: "Based on +15 years of rigorous academic research"; "Adopted by thousands of companies and institutes worldwide"; "we've rigorously studied and worked with hundreds of startups" (site claims, unaudited). Blank: "Academic research published in Management Science investigated 85 VC-backed startups and offered a conclusion that seems obvious in hindsight: 'look before you leap.' The big idea was that experienced entrepreneurs tend to generate a portfolio of market opportunities before deciding where to play, thereby laying the ground for significant performance benefits." *(verified as Blank's statement; the underlying paper was not read)*
  - Working paper (Tal, Gruber and de Haan): "an in-depth, qualitative investigation of 25 new technology ventures" (Israeli imaging and image-processing ventures; "32 semi-structured face-to-face interviews"). It identifies "three distinct approaches to market entry: focused, broad and vague". Findings: "the majority of new ventures (72% of firms in our sample) had to adapt their initial market entry decision during the first three years". "ventures with a broad approach tend to outperform others on all key performance dimensions. These ventures raised, on average, 7.38 million USD from external investors ... only 18% of these ventures searched for additional funds at the end of year three, compared to 66% and 60% of the focused and vague types, respectively. Ventures led by a broad approach also had a much greater number of employees (18.8 (broad), 5.75 (focused), 4.6 (vague))." The broad approach "encourages the commitment of actions to a set of alternatives –which, as our data indicate, tend to be closely related"; "These dual abilities – to commit and to be flexible at the same time – increase these ventures' chances of establishing themselves as viable organizations relatively more quickly". *(verified)* *Reader inference:* small, single-country, single-technology qualitative sample; association, not causation; the working paper's venue and peer-review status are unknown.
- Key quotes: Flyability case (Blank): "five market domains seemed interesting and required further evaluation" → "Gradually, it became clear that thermal power plants were a 'gold mine' option worth playing in" → "the founders designed a small portfolio of backup and growth options that they would keep open. This foresight laid the ground to early key decisions that have long-term consequences, like how they developed their drone or chose their brand name." Site: "Make it a habit whenever you need to revisit your strategy." *(verified)*

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:* R40 is the complete identify-evaluate-prioritise loop the project needs, and it adds one design element the other sources lack. The output is a primary sub-market plus deliberately kept backup and growth options, with the rest parked. The working paper gives modest empirical support for that element: 72% of ventures had to adapt, and broad-but-related entry did better. This directly tempers Aulet's "burn the boats" (R16). For freight, the backups should be sub-markets that share the same software core and data, e.g. adjacent commodities on the same mode, or the same shipper group's other mode, so a re-start is cheap. The two-axis Attractiveness Map (potential vs challenge) is structurally the same as R12/R14. "Challenge" (difficulty and time to capture value) is not the same thing as R12's "business strength relative to dominant players", and for a pre-revenue team it is arguably the more honest axis. Before the process doc hard-codes the potential/challenge sub-criteria, someone should download the free worksheets (email sign-up) to verify them; it takes minutes.
