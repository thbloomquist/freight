# R30 - 'It's not sexy to talk about a grain elevator but it's required': Bushel's Jake Joraanstad on 10 years of agtech evolution

- Decision the resource addresses: beachhead or niche choice (which node of the grain supply chain to sell software to), problem discovery before commitment (stated in the negative, as the "solutions looking for problems" failure of the first agtech cycle), and, indirectly, vertical selection (broadacre grain and oilseeds, US). It is a founder's retrospective account, not a formal method.

## Strategies or frameworks described

### "The messy middle" - target the middle of the supply chain as the most underserved node (author's phrase)
- Origin and intended context: B2B agtech software startup (Bushel, launched 2017; US broadacre grain and oilseeds; customers are grain elevators, ag retailers, co-ops, "the big grain company"). Told in hindsight in 2023.
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim, author's ordering):
  - Journalist's framing: "No one had yet realized that trying to sell agtech directly to farmers was an inefficient way to digitize the industry."
  - JJ: "We realized that there was nobody really focused on what we call 'the messy middle' of the supply chain. There had been 10 years of focus on farmer-facing tools and selling things to the farmer. For us, it was a realization that going direct to the farmer hasn't worked, and finding a path to go to market as a software company. And we realized that the middle of the supply chain - the agribusiness, the co-op, the retailer, the big grain company, etc. - is the most underserved and that's been our core focus from day one." (section "AFN: In the last few years, what are the most important developments you've seen at Bushel?")
  - Why direct-to-farmer failed (section "AFN: Why has it been so difficult to sell directly to farmers? Why haven't other agtechs addressed this 'messy middle'?"): "For a long time, nobody knew [selling direct to farmers] wasn't the best route, and it took some time for people to realize. Part of the challenge is traveling out into these farms, which are generally outside of the city, and going direct one to one to a farmer. The cost of acquisition in a traditional sales model, whether you're selling anything at all, but certainly in software, it's maybe too high."
  - Why the middle was left open: "The other part is, it's not sexy to jump in and talk about a grain elevator, right? You have to be really nuanced and understand the details of how our culture works, at least in the broadacre world of grain and oilseeds. There's just so few software investments, for example, in that technology space of the middle part in these last 10 years. It's been minimal until more recently, and so that's the opportunity we wanted to uncover. But it was unsexy. We're doing the plumbing, so to speak, and nobody wants to be the plumber. But it's required to make things work."
- Criteria, inputs and data required (as stated, all qualitative): customer acquisition cost of the direct-to-farmer sales model (dispersed rural customers, one-to-one selling); degree to which a node is "underserved" (proxy: how few software investments have gone there); whether the node is "unsexy" (a reason competitors avoid it); whether the team has the cultural nuance to work in it. No CAC figures or counts of investments are given.
- Output produced: choice of the buyer/handler node (elevators, retailers, co-ops, grain companies) as the customer, with farmers reached through that node's tools ("The Bushel mobile app").
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: stated: applies "at least in the broadacre world of grain and oilseeds"; the founder concedes the lesson was learned slowly. Reader inference: single-founder, self-reported and promotional; survivorship (Bushel's own choice is the example); "the middle" here means the buying/handling intermediaries, not carriers, so it does not map one-to-one onto the project's carrier side; the source gives no numbers for the CAC claim.
- Key quotes: see above; also the headline quote "It's not sexy to jump in and talk about a grain elevator ... We're doing the plumbing, so to speak, and nobody wants to be the plumber. But it's required to make things work."

### "Solutions looking for problems" - the anti-pattern of the first agtech cycle (author's phrase)
- Origin and intended context: agtech 2013-2023, Silicon Valley capital investing in Midwest agriculture.
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim): "Back then, 'agtech' was largely a bunch of 'solutions looking for problems,' he tells AgFunderNews." "The other thing we had to get through were the growing pains of building software that was a solution looking for a problem. There was a lot of that in the first 10 years. A lot of Silicon Valley money was coming in to invest in companies that are supposed to know agriculture in the Midwest. That cultural gap was a challenge. There wasn't a lot of receptiveness to Silicon Valley Tech coming in." (section "AFN: Have we adequately addressed the digital adoption issue?") On the consequence (section "AFN: What's one thing ag should have done differently in the last 10 years?"): "Investing in solutions looking for problems. We're seeing the outcome of that now: very few companies have returned capital. ... As an investor if you're coming into the space, your assumption is your batting average is going to be really low."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: whether a product starts from an observed problem of a reachable customer; whether the team knows the culture.
- Output produced: a warning rather than a procedure.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: no data beyond the assertion that "very few companies have returned capital".
- Key quotes: as above.

### "Plumbing" / digital-infrastructure readiness as a precondition (author's phrase)
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim): "Also, 10 years ago, there was not really any 'plumbing' put in. All the tools we wanted to build are so manual and so intensive in terms of input of information, that there wasn't good adoption at all. A spreadsheet could do the same thing. When we started in 2017, none of the Ag ERPs that are a big part of this space had an API. Today most of them are developing or have built APIs, but that was how behind we were with the connectivity and working with other companies building software." Also the digital-adoption framing: "Everybody 10 years ago would have said digital adoption; the farmer was not ready for technology ... So the core understanding of our challenge was, 'How do we build technology that farmers will use?' And how do we build a business out of that?" and "Ten years ago, McKinsey came out with that first farming report that said agriculture is the least digitized industry of all the major industries in the world. I'm pretty certain that it's still true today".
- Criteria: existence of APIs on the incumbent systems of record (ag ERPs); whether the product needs manual data entry that a spreadsheet could replace.
- Output: a readiness judgement on a candidate node.
- Reader inference: the same precondition R28 states as "digital infrastructure ... a prerequisite"; a sub-market whose systems of record have no APIs will force the entrant to build the plumbing first.

### Three "most pivotal changes" - timing / why-now conditions (section "AFN: What of these developments have been the most pivotal changes?")
- Verbatim: "There are three. One is that rural connectivity is largely solved. The next major change was the machinery. Companies like John Deere are leading the way, building real tools and tech software that works with their machines. ... The third one ... was the user experience. The bar for a former user experience in 2014 was really, really, really low. ... Today, there's the expectation to make [these tools] almost consumer-like in terms of ease of use."
- Reader inference: a checklist of enabling conditions (connectivity, machine data, UX expectations) that changes which nodes are addressable over time.

### The labor problem and harvest-to-elevator logistics (section "AFN: What's one problem that hasn't gone away?") - evidence, not a strategy
- Verbatim: "The labor problem. We're only going to have fewer people in agriculture. In the 1800s, we had a 90% of the population in ag. Today, it's 1.3%. ... Technology, and particularly software that helps create efficiencies for businesses, is one of the only ways you can replace that back office person who no longer lives in town, or a retiree, and we don't have a replacement. We have a lot of jobs that are not being filled, and we need to automate."
- Verbatim description of the grain handling chain: "If you want to combine corn today, you have something called the grain cart which has to meet the combine on the field to dump the corn out of it. Then that grain cart is pulled by a tractor. Another person has to drive it over to the semi, which has another person in it, and unload into the semi. Then the semi has to drive into town, wait in line potentially at the elevator, and then dump the grain and come back in time for that grain cart to load the next batch. If any of those things are out of sync, then you're waiting and you can't do anything. ... I think within the next three years, it will become common that this is a one person job."
- Reader inference (relevant to the origin hypothesis): the farm-side "shipping and handling" cost described here is labor and waiting time in a synchronisation problem (cart, tractor, semi, elevator queue), not a freight rate paid to a carrier; the source never says farmers lose profit to shipping.

### The wedge product and the outlook (section "AFN: What do the next 10 years look like?")
- Verbatim: "In the near term, there's going to be significant consolidation. For the first time ever, the farmer doesn't have to go and type in a paper contract into software. That's a big deal. ... Although those automation points may look little, when put in aggregate over time, they create an ecosystem that is going to be really powerful."
- Reader inference: Bushel's wedge is document/contract digitisation between elevator and farmer, i.e. the same "data entry" class of problem R28 lists.

### Examples, case studies and data sources used
- Bushel (own company; launched 2017; acquired FarmLogs), the Bushel mobile app, John Deere Ops Center, Tesla semi (speculative), "McKinsey ... first farming report" (agriculture least digitized of major industries; the interview dates it "ten years ago"), the 90% -> 1.3% ag population figures (uncited in the article), the AgFunder 10-year e-zine. ## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
Judgement: This is the strongest single piece of evidence in the batch against treating "farmers" as the customer even if the origin hypothesis about their costs turns out to be true: a founder who spent a decade in the space says direct-to-farmer software failed on acquisition cost and cultural fit, and that the reachable, underserved buyer is the handling intermediary (elevator, co-op, retailer, grain company). For Q1 that yields a clusterable heuristic: choose the node by (1) customer acquisition cost and reachability, (2) under-investment ("unsexy") as a proxy for low competition, (3) the team's insider knowledge, and (4) infrastructure readiness (APIs, "plumbing"), with "solutions looking for problems" as the failure mode to avoid. It only half-fits the project's two-sided definition: Bushel's "middle" is the buyer/handler side of the shipper's transaction, not the carrier, broker or 3PL side, so a freight sub-market built on this lesson would pair grain farmers (shippers) with elevators/co-ops as the receiving handler and would still need the carrier side filled in from other sources (e.g. the USDA grain transport and AgRTQ data in R31-R34). Treat all claims as one founder's self-report in a promotional trade interview, with no numbers behind the CAC assertion.
