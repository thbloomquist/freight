## Strategies or frameworks described

### "Market -> sub-market -> product-market -> actual consumer segments" (the author's "layered approach", section 10)
- Origin and intended context: B2C marketing teaching material for "students and practitioners" (the site's own blurb for the sister page calls it a guide for "university-level marketing students"). Written from the viewpoint of an established firm deciding "where to compete"; all worked examples are large incumbents (Coca-Cola, Toyota, Oracle, mid-sized banks). Not startup-specific. B2B appears only inside three examples (commercial trucks, ERP, corporate lending).
- Mechanism and steps (the author's ordering):
  1. Define the market. "A market is the broad, overarching space of exchange between buyers (consumers, end users) and sellers (firms, producers, service providers) for a given need or want." (2.1) A market "involves: Demand ... Supply ... Competitive Interactions: A set of direct and indirect competitors contending to satisfy these consumer needs."
  2. Break it into sub-markets. "A sub-market is a smaller slice of the overall market where the products, competitive environment, or customer usage differ significantly from the rest of the market. Each sub-market usually has: Unique offerings or product categories (e.g., an after-school tutoring sub-market within 'education'), Specialist players or unique distribution channels, Different pricing dynamics, cost structures, or promotional approaches." (2.2) "Hence, a sub-market represents a distinct part of the broader market, although it still shares an overarching need or theme."
  3. Define product-markets inside a chosen sub-market. "A product-market is a more targeted definition of 'where a firm competes,' pinpointing both a specific product type (or product line) and the particular market (or sub-market) in which it's sold. Think of it as the intersection between the sub-market chosen and the firm's specific offerings." (2.3) Geography is an optional third dimension: "organic juice" + "South America beverage market" (2.3, 4.2).
  4. Only then segment consumers within the sub-market (3.2, 9.1).
- Criteria, inputs and data required: qualitative knowledge of product categories, competitor sets ("specialist players"), distribution channels, pricing dynamics and cost structures, and customer usage. No data source, dataset or sizing method is named anywhere on the page.
- Output produced: a hierarchy: one market, a list of sub-markets, the firm's chosen product-market(s) (product line x sub-market x geography), then consumer segments. Section 3.1 says the sub-markets so listed "would have different sizes, operates quite differently, have different profitability and growth measures".
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (stated) The author frames sub-markets as supply-side categories: "Sub-markets revolve around product categories, whereas market segments revolve around customer needs or behaviors within that product category." (8.1); "Sub-markets break that large market into product-driven categories" (10). (reader inference) The definition itself names three differentiators, one of which is demand-side ("customer usage"), but the enumeration examples are all product/service categories; this is the gap the project's joint shipper+carrier definition fills. (reader inference) The enumeration is illustrative, not derived by any procedure: the body text lists seven education sub-markets ("potentially including" public K-12, private K-12, tutoring, universities/colleges, corporate training, online platforms, adult continuing education) while the page's own diagram (fig. 1, captioned "The education market and its sub-markets", source line www.marketingstudyguide.com) shows a different seven: Universities, Private schools, Religious schools, Vocational colleges, Adult education (hobbies and interests), Tutoring services, Online courses. No criterion is given for how many sub-markets to draw or where to stop.
- Key quotes: "By the end, you should have a robust understanding of how organizations conceptualize 'where to compete,' how sub-markets differ from the broader market, and how product-markets guide targeted marketing actions." (1) "In the beverage market, for example, 'juice' is a sub-market separate from 'carbonated soft drinks,' each with specialized competitors and consumer expectations." (2.2) "Each sub-market differs in size, operational models, and competitive players." (3.1) "Ultimately, this layered approach—market → sub-market → product-market → actual consumer segments—provides the fundamental structure for any solid marketing strategy." (10)

### Sequencing: identify sub-markets, then decide where to compete, then segment ("Integrating Market, Sub-Market, and Product-Market with STP", section 9)
- Origin and intended context: same as above; it is the author's bridge from market structure to the STP (segmentation, targeting, positioning) process.
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim, 9.1): "You cannot do meaningful segmentation on the entire 'beverage market' worldwide without losing practical insights. Instead, you first identify sub-markets (e.g., juice, carbonated soft drinks), then you define where you plan to compete (e.g., the 'juice sub-market in South America'), and then segment that sub-market into consumer groups (health-focused, budget-friendly, flavor adventurers, etc.)." Targeting (9.2): "Once the segments in a sub-market are established, you decide which are large or profitable enough or which align best with your brand." Positioning (9.3) follows per sub-market and product-market.
- Criteria, inputs and data required: the only selection criteria on the page, both one-liners: at segment level "large or profitable enough" and "align best with your brand" (9.2); at sub-market level the question "Which sub-markets are strategic priorities (due to growth or synergy with existing capabilities)?" (8.3). Dimensions on which sub-markets are said to differ, usable as comparison axes: size, operational model, competitive players, profitability, growth (3.1), consumer priorities, margins, competitor profiles (8.2).
- Output produced: the chosen sub-market and product-market scope; then a segment list; then a positioning statement per product-market.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (stated rationale for the sequence) practicality: "Without subdividing the beverage market into water, juice, carbonated drinks, etc., it would be impractical to do a single segmentation of the entire global beverage market." (4.3). (reader inference) No procedure, weights, thresholds or data are given for the "where to compete" step; the page assumes the firm already has products and a brand, so "synergy with existing capabilities" presupposes an incumbent. For a pre-product startup that criterion is empty and must be replaced.
- Key quotes: "Which sub-markets are strategic priorities (due to growth or synergy with existing capabilities)? Which product-markets they plan to compete in, across which geographies, with which product lines? How they can design subsequent segmentation and targeting processes (STP) to account for actual consumer differences." (8.3) "If you produce premium juices, you might target 'health-conscious, higher-income families,' ignoring the bargain-seeking or kids' flavors segments." (9.2)

### Avoiding product-centric "segments" (sections 3.2 and 8.1)
- Origin and intended context: a validity check the author applies throughout; teaching-oriented.
- Mechanism and steps: test each proposed "segment": if it is a product category it is a sub-market, not a segment. "These segments differ not by product categories, but by consumer usage, motivations, and goals." (3.2) "One major lesson is not to confuse a sub-market (like 'credit cards') with a true 'market segment.' ... For instance, 'frequent flyer rewards seekers' is a real market segment inside the credit card sub-market." (8.1)
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none beyond the definitions.
- Output produced: a cleaned list where the sub-market level is product/usage-defined and the segment level is customer-need-defined.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (reader inference) this is a check, not a selection method; but it is a direct guard against enumerating freight sub-markets solely from carrier service types.
- Key quotes: "Sub-markets revolve around product categories, whereas market segments revolve around customer needs or behaviors within that product category." (8.1)

### "Lifetime Customer Journey" (section 3.3)
- Origin and intended context: an observation about consumers moving between sub-markets over time.
- Mechanism and steps: "An important note is that an individual consumer may tap into multiple sub-markets over time—attending a private school as a teen, a university later, then using online platforms for professional training. This highlights how sub-markets remain linked under the umbrella of a single overarching market."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none.
- Output produced: none (conceptual).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: (reader inference) the freight analogue is that one shipper buys from several freight sub-markets at once (inbound inputs, outbound product, different modes), so shipper-based and carrier-service-based enumerations cross-cut and a two-sided sub-market must be defined at their intersection.
- Key quotes: as above.

- Education (sections 3.1-3.3): seven sub-markets (see the text/diagram discrepancy above); segments inside online education: test preparation, professional upskilling, casual learners; funding-model contrasts (private K-12 tuition and philanthropy vs public universities' government funding); Coursera, edX named.

- Automotive (5.1-5.3): sub-markets Electric Vehicles (Tesla, Rivian; "distinct distribution models (online reservations, specialized showrooms), unique consumer concerns (range anxiety, charging infrastructure)"), Luxury Vehicles (BMW, Mercedes, Lexus), Commercial Trucks ("Serve B2B customers, focusing on reliability, total cost of ownership, often sold via specialized fleet management channels"; "commercial truck sellers partner with logistic associations"); Toyota product-markets (hybrid sedans for eco-conscious families in North America; compact budget cars for emerging Asian markets; luxury crossover SUVs in Europe).
- Software (6.1-6.3): sub-markets CRM (Salesforce, HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics), ERP (SAP, Oracle), Project Management Tools (Trello, Asana, Monday.com), Cybersecurity; "Each sub-market often has specialized channels—like direct enterprise sales, subscription-based models, or channel partnerships—and distinct competitor sets."; Oracle product-markets (cloud ERP for mid-sized US manufacturers; DBaaS for large retailers globally; HR modules for small businesses in Asia-Pacific).

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
(Judgement.) This page supplies the project's working definition and the sequencing rule (enumerate sub-markets, decide where to compete, then segment) but nothing that could be called a selection method: its only criteria are "growth or synergy with existing capabilities", "large or profitable enough" and brand fit, none of them operationalised, and the "synergy with capabilities" test presumes an incumbent with a product. Its most useful transferable content is the definition's three differentiators (products, competitive environment, customer usage), which already straddle the carrier side (products, competitors, channels, pricing) and the shipper side (usage), and the warning that a list of product categories is not a list of customer groups, which argues against enumerating freight sub-markets from carrier service types (FTL, LTL, reefer, intermodal, forwarding) alone. The commercial-trucks example is the page's only freight-adjacent content and it treats trucks as a product sold to fleets, not as a freight service. Treat R01 as the vocabulary and the ordering of steps; look to R12/R14 (scoring) and R05-R07 (definition axes) for the actual choosing.
