- Decision the resource addresses: candidate enumeration (Step 1A brainstorm), segment or sub-market evaluation and scoring (Step 1B narrow to 4-10, Step 1C matrix), and problem discovery before commitment (primary market research to fill the matrix). Beachhead choice itself is deferred to Step 2 (R16).

## Strategies or frameworks described

### Definition of a market segment: three characteristics ("Three Key Criteria to Be Met If You Are to Create a New Market That You Will Dominate")
- Origin and intended context: innovation-driven startups (technology push or market pull), pre-product, MIT "New Enterprises" class and the author's own ventures (SensAble Technologies). Aimed at founders "creating new markets" rather than "me-too" entrants; both B2C and B2B examples appear. *(verified)*
- Mechanism: "A target customer or 'market segment' is a group of potential customers who share three fundamental characteristics: 1. They buy the same product. 2. They buy it in the same way (i.e., same use case, value proposition, channel). 3. There is word of mouth (WOM) of some sort between them; that is to say, that they significantly influence each other. A market segment should have all three characteristics and not just the first one." (p. 15)
- Criteria, inputs: qualitative judgement about product, buying process/channel and WOM, to be validated by primary market research (PMR).
- Output: a test of whether a proposed candidate is one segment or several.
- Assumptions, limitations (stated): "Relying on gut feel, anecdotes, or third-party analysis is basically guesswork and is insufficient if you are creating new markets in a disciplined manner" (p. 15). Reader inference: the unit of analysis is the end user and their use case, not an industry or a value-chain pairing.
- Key quote (p. 15): "You will have to be relentlessly focused on a well-defined, winnable, and appropriately sized initial target customer (i.e., not too big and not too small) that you can grow your business from."

### Two-sided or multi-sided markets and primary vs. secondary customers ("Complex Paying Customers")
- Origin and intended context: same; examples Google (advertiser/end user), Uber (drivers/riders), a class venture matching artists with students.
- Mechanism: "To simplify things and to keep the process moving, you should note but not worry about this primary/secondary customer delineation and simply focus on the end user. If the end user does not gain value from the product, there will be no economic buyer." (p. 18) For multi-sided markets: "If you have a multi-sided market, you will complete each step of the 24 Steps once for each side of the market. But you will likely find through your PMR that one side of the market is harder to establish and therefore more critical to win for your business to succeed. I recommend that you start by focusing your efforts on the more difficult side first. It is usually on the demand side, but not always." (pp. 18-19)
- Output: a rule for sequencing the analysis of the two sides.
- Limitations: none stated beyond "start with the harder side". Reader inference: Aulet's "multi-sided" means a platform needing both sides as customers; the project's shipper/carrier definition of a sub-market is related but not identical (only one side may be the paying customer).

### Step 1A: Brainstorm
- Origin and intended context: same; team exercise; explicitly for people who "already have my idea".
- Mechanism (verbatim, p. 19-20): "Do not focus on viability or the solution at this point; rather, focus on getting as long a list as possible of potential people who would benefit from your idea. More is better at this stage. Let a thousand flowers bloom! You will narrow it down later in the process." "Often some of the most interesting initial or what you will call Beachhead Markets in Step 2 exist on the fringe of the spectrum and are overlooked by others, so think broadly." "No idea is a bad idea. I encourage my students to use improvisation techniques where the first tenet is always to say 'yes' to any idea." "talking about your idea or technology with potential customers even at this early stage will give you clear and accurate feedback ... forget the unenlightened idea of 'stealth mode.'" "the goal in this stage is to open the aperture as far as possible".
- Inputs: the idea/technology (or, for market pull, the problem); team; early conversations with potential customers. SensAble's idea sources (p. 34): "our current customers, trade show feedback, incoming product inquiries, and our own imaginations".
- Output: a long list of potential end users and applications.
- Limitations: unstructured; the chapter refers to "Worksheet 1.1: Structure for Brainstorming Market Segments" on d-eship.com (not read).

### Step 1B: Narrow ("voting ideas off the island" with a sequence of filters, then eight criteria)
- Origin and intended context: same; Aulet credits Geoffrey Moore's *Inside the Tornado* (Documentum narrowed 80 potential markets with five criteria) and expands to eight.
- Mechanism (author's ordering, pp. 20-23):
  1. "start by voting ideas 'off the island' ... Look at the ideas you have with a critical eye, based on a set of filters, to eliminate the ones you feel are not a good fit."
  2. Filter order: "People usually start with analytical market analysis filters (e.g., How big is the market? How strong is the competition? How strong is our value proposition? What is the ability of the customer to pay? How long will it take? What types of resources will I need?). While all of these are good questions, none of them are the first question you should ask. The first question you should ask is a personal filter question: Is this market segment one I could get really, really excited about and dedicate the next six years of my life to serving? Will I be proud to be associated with serving this marketplace? If not, don't do it."
  3. "after the personal filter, you can apply the business filter, which is focused on the competitive and strategic considerations of the markets. In the next filter, which I call the external filter, you will consider how many additional resources you will need to achieve your goal in the marketplace and whether it is attainable. This includes teammates, partners, and capital. The final filter I list is the execution filter, which is a risk assessment of the market. How many things need to go right for you to win this market segment, and what are the odds of all of them being successful?" (Figure 1.3, p. 21, draws this as a funnel whose bands read, top to bottom: PERSONAL, MARKET OPPORTUNITY, PRODUCT VIABILITY, EXECUTION, COMPETITION, RESOURCES; the figure's labels are more numerous than the four filters named in the text. *(verified by viewing the page image)*)
  4. The eight criteria (p. 22-23), quoted in full:
     "1. Is the target customer well-funded? If the customer does not have money, the market is not attractive because it will not be sustainable and provide positive cash flow for the new venture to grow.
     2. Is the target customer readily accessible to your sales force? You want to deal directly with customers when starting out, rather than rely on third parties to market and sell your product, because your product will go through iterations of improvement rapidly, and direct customer feedback is an essential part of that process. ...
     3. Does the target customer have a compelling reason to buy? Would the customer buy your product instead of another similar solution? Or is the customer content with whatever solution is already being used? Remember that on many occasions, your primary competition will be the customer doing nothing.
     4. Can you today, with the help of partners, deliver a whole product? ... They want to buy a whole functional solution, not assemble one themselves. ...
     5. Is there entrenched competition that could block you? ... How strong are those competitors, from the customer's viewpoint (not your viewpoint or from a technical standpoint)? Can the competition block you from starting a business relationship with a customer? ...
     6. If you win this segment, can you leverage it to enter additional segments? ... Geoffrey Moore uses the metaphor of a bowling alley, where the Beachhead Market is the lead pin ...
     7. Is the market consistent with the values, passions, and goals of the founding team? ...
     8. How quickly can you win this market? You have to find markets that you can win in a reasonable time frame consistent with your personal timelines."
  5. Level of analysis: "Start by asking these questions at an industry level but don't spend too much time at that level. What really matters is the end user and the specific job they are doing." (p. 23)
  6. Count: "Four to 10 good market segment opportunities are sufficient (10 is too many in most cases and usually 4 to 6 is a good range)." (p. 23)
  7. Process rule: "Each time, before you eliminate a market segment, allow time for the group to discuss ... Lastly, once the obvious candidates are removed, have the group vote on the remaining market segments to get a prioritized list." (p. 23)
- Criteria, inputs and data required: qualitative team judgement on the filters and eight criteria; "In this section, you will not be as rigorous as you will be in Step 2 when you narrow the field of 4-10 down to one."
- Output: a prioritised shortlist of 4-10 (ideally 4-6) market segments.
- Assumptions, limitations (stated): the personal filter is deliberately placed first ("You will not last"); rigour is deferred to Step 2. Reader inference: no weighting or scoring scheme is given here; the eight criteria are the same ones used in Step 2 (see R16), so this is a lightweight pre-pass of the beachhead criteria.

### Step 1C: Build a Market Segmentation Matrix and fill it with Primary Market Research
- Origin and intended context: same; the template is the book's central Step 1 deliverable (digital copy at www.d-eship.com, Worksheet 1.2).
- Mechanism: "build a framework to analyze the top market segments in order to organize your research. Balance comprehensiveness with the benefits of simplicity." (p. 23) The 17 starter rows and their definitions (Figure 1.4, pp. 24-26), condensed but keeping the author's wording:
  1. Market Segment Name: "Carefully name the market segment so it appropriately and precisely captures the group you want and no more".
  2. End User: "the person who is actually using the product, not the economic buyer or the champion".
  3. Task: "What exactly is it that the end users do that you will significantly affect or allow them to do that they could not do before?"
  4. Benefit: "What is the benefit that you believe the end users will get?"
  5. Urgency of Need: "What is the level of urgency to solve the problem or capture the new opportunity for the end user?"
  6. Example End Users: "examples of end users that you can, have, or will talk to, so as to validate your perceptions".
  7. Lead Customers: "the influential customers (i.e., lighthouse customers) where, if they buy the product, others will take note and likely follow".
  8. Willingness to Change: "How conservative is this market segment? How open are they to change? Is there something to force change (e.g., impending crisis)?"
  9. Frequency of Buying: "How often do they buy new products? What does their buying cycle look like at a high level?"
  10. Concentration of Buyers: "How many different buyers are there in this market segment? Is it a monopoly? Oligopoly (a small number of buyers)? Or many competitive buyers?"
  11. Other Relevant Market Considerations: e.g. "'high employee turnover,' 'very low margins/commodity,' 'high-growth industry,' 'high virality effect' (i.e., word of mouth)".
  12. Size of Market (# of End Users): "to a relevant range (10s, 100s, 1Ks, 10Ks, 100Ks, 1Ms, etc.)".
  13. Est. Value of End User ($1, $10, $100, $1K, etc.): "A first-pass estimate of the value of each end user, again to a relevant order of magnitude".
  14. Competition/Alternatives: "What will be your competition from the end users' perspective? Include the 'do nothing' option as well."
  15. Other Components Needed for a Full Solution: "the complementary assets that you do not currently have but would need to build or acquire to give the end user a full solution".
  16. Important Partners: "the partners or distributors you will have to work with to fit into the end user's workflow ... or business processes".
  17. Other Relevant Personal Considerations: "where the market segment is geographically centered, whether it matches the values of the founding team, existing knowledge and contacts in market, etc."
  Optional extra rows: "regulatory environment, special resource requirements or constraints, or macro-trends" (p. 26).
  Filling the matrix: "it is time to do primary market research (PMR) ... PMR is when you directly interact with your potential market segment, the end user, but also the other stakeholders in that ecosystem." (p. 26-27) "While you should find out what you can about customers and markets via secondary market research before you talk to potential customers, it is impossible to overstate the importance of doing direct customer research." (p. 27) Modes: "speaking with them (i.e., interviewing), watching them as they work (i.e., primary observational research), doing the job yourself (i.e., immersion), seeing their responses to carefully designed tests (i.e., A/B testing), or following their digital dust" (p. 27). Posture: "inquiry mode," not "advocacy mode"; three caveats: "1. You do not have 'the answer' for your potential customers and their needs. 2. Your potential customers do not have 'the answer' for you. 3. Talk with potential customers in 'inquiry' mode, not 'advocacy/sales' mode." (p. 28)
  Time budget: "at least a few weeks (and maybe much longer if your situation permits)"; SensAble spent "over three months"; "Somewhere around 90% of the data in the chart came from direct interaction ... Very little data came from reports by well-known research firms or by finding data on the Internet." (pp. 28, 35)
- Criteria, inputs and data required: interviews, observation, immersion, tests; order-of-magnitude sizing; the rows above.
- Output: a filled comparison matrix across the 4-10 candidates, used to select a Beachhead Market in Step 2; plus "A mindset change" (customer-back rather than product-out) and "The beginnings of a coherent and cohesive team" (p. 29).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques (stated): "You will not find a singular perfect market segment - this is not a math problem with a deterministic singular answer." (p. 28) "Do not let yourself fall into 'analysis paralysis.'" (p. 29) Secondary research is distrusted for new markets: "If there is already a market research report out there with all the information you need, it is probably too late for your new venture." (p. 27) Pitfalls named: "selling to everyone" and "the abstract paying customer scenario" / "fun with spreadsheets" / "small percentage of a big market syndrome" (pp. 16-18). Reader inference: the matrix is a comparison template, not a scoring model; no weights or thresholds are given, and the final choice mechanism lives in Step 2.

### Market-pull segmentation dimensions (Gradeable example)
- Origin and intended context: "market pull," where "you have a problem and you need to find a solution"; example is a grading tool for public-school teachers.
- Mechanism: "You can start with segmenting in many different dimensions and there is no singular order that is correct." (p. 38) Figure 1.10 dimensions x options: Subject (Math, Science, Language Arts, Social Studies, Other); Level (Pre-K, Elementary, Middle, High School, Other); Type of School (Public, Independent, Religious, Vocational, Other); Geography #1 (Massachusetts, New York, Connecticut, New Hampshire, Other); Geography #2 (Urban, Suburban, Exurban, Rural, Other); Experience of Teacher (0-5, 5-10, 10-15, 15+ years, Other); Receptivity to Technology (Technological Enthusiast, Early Adopter, Early Majority, Late Majority, Laggard); "More to the specific situation ...". "Note that they customized the various dimensions greatly to better reflect the data that would help them segment their markets."
- Output: a dimension grid from which concrete segments are composed.
- Key quote (p. 39): "Even if some might have similar needs and want exactly the same product, they will have different circumstances and how they have to sell to them will vary, and that will make all the difference."

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:* This is the most operational enumerate-then-narrow procedure in the batch: brainstorm wide, gate first on team fit, then apply business/external/execution filters and the eight criteria to reach 4-10 candidates, then compare them in a 17-row matrix filled mostly by talking to people. Several matrix rows map directly onto the project's sub-market questions (Task, Urgency of Need, Willingness to Change, Concentration of Buyers, Competition/Alternatives including "do nothing", Other Components Needed for a Full Solution, Important Partners), and the explicit rule for multi-sided markets ("complete each step ... once for each side ... start by focusing your efforts on the more difficult side first") is the only guidance in this batch that speaks to the shipper/carrier two-sidedness. Its limits for this project: the unit is the end user and their job, so a freight sub-market (shipper group x carrier type) must be re-expressed as end users on each side; the filters and criteria are unweighted qualitative judgements; and the author's insistence that most data come from primary research runs against a desk-research-first plan for Q2, although he does allow secondary research before interviews. The "fee out of savings" test is not among his criteria and would need to be added as a knock-out row.
