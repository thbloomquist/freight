- Decision the resource addresses: market definition, meaning how to define a market in problem space so it stays stable while the product, vertical or technology changes. It is also a step toward candidate enumeration and problem discovery.

## Strategies or frameworks described

### Diagnosis: why product-, vertical- or technology-based market definitions trap start-ups
- Origin and intended context: "aspiring entrepreneurs" and innovators, B2C and B2B. *(verified)*
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim): "entrepreneurs use various, seemingly random classification schemes to define the markets they serve ... around a product ... around verticals ... demographics ... technologies ... customer activities ... and product portfolios". "Here's the problem: Depending on how they originally define their market, making one or more of these changes can inadvertently alter the original market definition, which in turn changes the 'market' they are targeting and invalidates the needs they have captured. Here's the consequence: They enter a recursive process in which they are iterating on the market definition, customer needs, and the value proposition simultaneously, with no logical way to exit." Rule: "Innovators don't create markets; they create products to serve markets. Thus, the market must be defined and validated in the innovation equation before moving to needs discovery and product definition." *(verified)*
- Criteria, inputs and data required: none; this is the problem statement.
- Output produced: the requirement that the market definition be fixed before needs discovery.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: opinion from the method's author, who sells consulting around it (Strategyn). *(reader inference)*
- Rationale and evidence base: the rationale is avoiding the "recursive process ... with no logical way to exit". Author authority; no data.
- Key quotes: as above.

### Eight tests of a good market definition
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim list): "A market should be defined in such a way that… It becomes a constant in the product/market fit equation, not a variable. It does not change as the study of that market unfolds. It is stable over time. It does not go away when different solutions or technologies come along ... It is unique from any other market, making it distinguishable and unambiguous. It does not assume a product or a solution. Rather, it is defined in problem space. It indicates who the targets are for value creation — making it clear which group of people to focus on. It makes the discovery of customer needs quicker, more effective, and less costly. It reveals all sources of competition, making disruption and other surprises less likely. It is relevant to and aligns the entire organization". *(verified)*
- Output produced: a quality checklist for any candidate market definition.
- Rationale and evidence base: "We've spent a good number of years asking ourselves what constitutes the 'perfect' market definition. This is what we concluded" (author experience).

### Definition: "a group of people and the job they are trying to get done", plus the 8-step JTBD Market Definition Canvas
- Mechanism and steps: "According to Jobs Theory, people buy products and services to get a 'job' done. A job is defined as a task people are trying to accomplish, a goal or objective they are trying to achieve, a problem they are trying to resolve, something they are trying to avoid". "a market is best defined as: a group of people and the job they are trying to get done." The canvas steps (verbatim headings with key instructions):
  1. "Traditional market definition": "What is the product/service/idea you seek to innovate?"
  2. "Job executor determination": "list all the categories of people who use or would use the product ... Do not list out influencers, economic buyers, people who support the product throughout its lifecycle, or other customer types, just job executors."
  3. "Abstracted job executor": "one overarching term ... try not to use an actual job title".
  4. "Job executor": final choice (e.g. "surgeons over cardiac surgeons"); "define the 'group of people' before defining the job-to-be-done, as you will be interviewing representatives of the group".
  5. "Function of the product": "What does/will the product or service we have in mind help you accomplish from a functional perspective?"
  6. "Other products used and their functions": "what other products they use immediately before, while, and immediately after using your product/service."
  7. "Abstracted job statement": "define your customer's job-to-be-done in a way that includes your product's function (job) and rationalizes why customers are using all these other products as they cobble together a complete solution." "complete steps 5–8 employing customer interviews ... If the job of the product is not represented, you have abstracted the job statement to too high a level."
  8. "Customer's Job-to-be-Done": document the final job statement; "work with job executors to gain consensus".
  Result: "Your market = Group of people (Step 4) + Job-to-be-Done (Step 8)". *(verified)*
- Two-sided use (verbatim): "While it is optimized to define single-sided markets, it can be used twice to define both sides of a double-sided market. For component manufacturers who sell to OEMs or who are at the top of a long distribution chain, a canvas can be completed for each constituent in the distribution chain, including the end-user, as each constituent has its own unique job to get done." *(verified)*
- Criteria, inputs and data required: interviews with job executors (steps 5-8); lists of adjacent products.
- Output produced: a problem-space market definition; per the author, "a built-in path and vision for growth — tied directly to what customers are trying to accomplish."
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: *reader inference:* the canvas deliberately excludes economic buyers, but a fee-from-savings product must also know who pays. The canvas assumes a product idea exists (step 1), so for a pre-product search it must be started from a candidate shipper activity instead. There is no sizing or selection among markets; it defines a market, it does not choose one.
- Rationale and evidence base: stability ("a valid long-term focal point for value creation") and cheaper needs discovery. Author authority; the essay cites no studies.
- Key quotes: as above.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:* R18 gives a way to define freight candidates that will not shift under us as the product idea changes. Each side is defined by its job, not by mode or service. These are reader examples, not source content:
- Shipper side: "perishable-goods shippers + get product from packing shed to buyer on time, in spec, at lowest landed cost".
- Freight-company side: "small reefer carriers + keep trucks loaded with paying freight and get paid quickly".

That also answers R01/R02's warning about product-category sub-markets. The author explicitly allows the canvas to be run twice for a double-sided market, and once per constituent of a distribution chain (shipper, broker/3PL, carrier, consignee). That fits the project's two-sided definition and the multi-party freight chain better than any other method in this half. The "reveals all sources of competition" test matters for a software entrant, whose real competitors are spreadsheets, phones, brokers and incumbent TMSs. The exclusion of economic buyers is the gap to fill for the fee constraint.
