- Decision the resource addresses: segment or sub-market evaluation and scoring (the target-segment selection step of a ten-step segmentation analysis). ## Strategies or frameworks described

### Step 8 "The Targeting Decision": knock-out criteria check, then weighted attractiveness scoring on two axes, then a segment evaluation plot (decision matrix)
- Origin and intended context: academic textbook on data-driven (survey/cluster-based) market segmentation analysis, written for practitioners and students. Examples are B2C (tourism: destination marketing, "BIG SPENDING CITY TOURIST", a "nature based destination in outback Australia") and non-profit (volunteer recruitment for land regeneration). Generic as to industry and firm size; assumes an organisation with a "segmentation team" and an "advisory committee" that has already run Steps 1-7 (Step 2 = deciding the ideal target segment, with knock-out criteria and weighted attractiveness criteria fixed *before* any data are collected; Step 5 = extracting segments; Step 6 = profiling; Step 7 = describing). Not startup-specific; no B2B example in this chapter. *(verified: chapter text)*
- Mechanism and steps (author's ordering, from section 10.1, 10.2 and the Step 8 Checklist in 10.3):
  1. "Convene a segmentation team meeting."
  2. "Determine which of the market segments profiled in Step 6 and described in Step 7 are being considered as potential target markets."
  3. "Double check that all of those remaining segments comply with the knock-out criteria of homogeneity, distinctness, size, match, identifiability and reachability. If a segment does not comply: eliminate it from further consideration." (Section 10.1 explains that "the knock-out criteria have already been applied in previous steps" optimally, so this is a re-check: size/homogeneity/distinctness would have shown in Step 6, identifiability/reachability in Step 7, and "needs the organisation cannot satisfy" in both.)
  4. "Discuss and agree on values for each market segment for each segment attractiveness criterion."
  5. "Discuss and agree on values for each relative organisational competitiveness criterion for each of the market segments."
  6. "Calculate each segment's overall attractiveness by multiplying the segment value with the weight for each criterion and then summing up all these values for each segment."
  7. "Calculate each segment's overall relative organisational competitiveness by multiplying the segment value with the weight for each criterion and then summing up all these values for each segment."
  8. "Plot the values onto a segment evaluation plot." (x-axis "How attractive is the segment to us?", y-axis "How attractive are we to the segment?", bubble size = a third criterion such as profit potential.)
  9. "Make a preliminary selection."
  10. "If you intend to target more than one segment: make sure that the selected target segments are compatible with one another."
  11. "Present the selected segments to the advisory committee for discussion and (if required) reconsideration."
  The whole step is framed by two questions (section 10.1): "1. Which of the market segments would the organisation most like to target? Which segment would the organisation like to commit to? 2. Which of the organisations offering the same product would each of the segments most like to buy from? How likely is it that our organisation would be chosen? How likely is it that each segment would commit to us?" *(verified)*
- Criteria, inputs and data required:
  - Knock-out criteria (six, named in the checklist): homogeneity, distinctness, size, match (needs the organisation can satisfy), identifiability, reachability. These are fixed in Step 2 and applied as pass/fail.
  - Segment attractiveness criteria and their weights: "specified in Step 2 of the market segmentation analysis. Step 2 resulted in a number of criteria of segment attractiveness, and weights quantifying how much impact each of these criteria has on the total value of segment attractiveness." The chapter does not list the criteria themselves (they are user-chosen). Weights are percentages summing to 100%.
  - Values per segment per criterion: "each segment is given a rating from 1 to 10 with 1 representing the worst and 10 representing the best value", assigned by the segmentation team "based on the profiles and descriptions resulting from Steps 6 and 7".
  - Relative organisational competitiveness criteria: chosen by asking "Which criteria do consumers use to select between alternative offers in the market?" The chapter offers examples: "attractiveness of the product to the segment in view of the benefits segment members seek; suitability of the current price to segment willingness or ability to pay; availability of distribution channels to get the product to the segment; segment awareness of the existence of the organisation or brand image of the organisation held by segment members."
  - Bubble-size variable: "Anything can be plotted onto the bubble size. Typically profit potential is plotted. Profit combines information about the size of the segment with spending".
  - Worked example, Table 10.1 (hypothetical, 8 segments): attractiveness weights 25%/35%/20%/10%/10%, competitiveness weights 25%/25%/25%/15%/10%; e.g. "Looking at segment 1 ... 0.25 · 5 + 0.35 · 2 + 0.20 · 10 + 0.10 · 8 + 0.10 · 9 = 5.65". Attractiveness totals 5.65, 5.05, 2.05, 5.25, 8.95, 4.25, 2.15, 9.6; competitiveness totals 3.7, 9.5, 6.55, 7.3, 2.2, 4.15, 2.35, 8.9; sizes 2.25, 5.25, 6.00, 3.75, 5.25, 2.25, 4.50, 1.50.
  - Tooling: R package MSA, `decisionMatrix(x, y, wx, wy, size = size)`.
- Output produced: a segment evaluation plot (bubble chart of attractiveness vs. relative competitiveness, bubble = profit potential or other) that "serves as a useful basis for discussions in the segmentation team", and a preliminary selection of "one or a small number" of target segments, checked for mutual compatibility and reviewed by an advisory committee. The reading of the example plot shows the decision logic: eliminate segments low on both axes even if large (segments 3 and 7); a segment highly attractive to the firm but not fond of the firm is "unlikely" to be won now (segment 5); the best match may be small (segment 8) so a second segment (2) that "loves the organisation, has decent profit potential" is added.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques (as stated): "there is no single best measure of segment attractiveness or relative organisational competitiveness. It is therefore necessary for users to return to their specifications of what an ideal target segment looks like for them." All cell values are team judgements, not measurements. The chapter lists many variants of the decision matrix ("Boston matrix ... General Electric / McKinsey matrix ... directional policy matrix ... McDonald four-box directional policy matrix ... market attractiveness-business strength matrix") and says "It is up to the market segmentation team to decide which variation of the decision matrix offers the most useful framework". Selection is described as long-term and binding: "The selection of one or more target segments is a long-term decision significantly affecting the future performance of an organisation."
  Reader inference: (a) the procedure presupposes an already-enumerated set of candidate segments produced by clustering respondent data; it gives no help in enumerating candidates. (b) The criteria and weights must exist before scoring (Step 2), so the real methodological work for a sub-market choice is in defining those criteria, which this chapter delegates elsewhere. (c) The knock-out criteria (size, homogeneity, distinctness, identifiability, reachability, match) are the same "segment viability" tests found in the classic textbook lists; nothing here is specific to B2B or two-sided markets.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:* This chapter supplies the cleanest generic scoring mechanic in the batch (knock-out gate, then weight × rating on two axes, then a plot for discussion), and it can be transplanted to sub-market selection by treating each candidate shipper-group × carrier-type pairing as a "segment" and fixing the knock-out criteria in advance (for this project: software-improvable problem, measurable cost, fee can be a share of savings). Its two-axis logic is organisation-versus-segment ("how attractive is the segment to us" / "how attractive are we to the segment"), not shipper-versus-carrier, so it does not by itself model the two-sided sub-market definition; both sides of a freight sub-market would have to be folded into the criteria list or scored as separate rows. The values are entirely team judgement, which the source acknowledges, so at the earliest research stage the method mainly disciplines the discussion rather than replacing evidence. It offers nothing for enumerating candidates or discovering problems before commitment (Q2); those must come from other resources.
