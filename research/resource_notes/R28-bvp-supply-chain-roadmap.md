# R28 - Roadmap: Supply Chain Software
- URL: https://www.bvp.com/atlas/roadmap-supply-chain-software | Author: Mike Droesch and Alice Deng, Bessemer Venture Partners (BVP "Atlas") | Year: 2021 (published 1 Dec 2021) | Type: primary (VC investment roadmap / thesis) | Access: free, read in full from the live page (raw HTML downloaded 2026-09-13 to scratchpad `r28_bvp.html`, text in `r28_bvp.txt`) | Accessed: 2026-09-13
- Decision the resource addresses: vertical selection (why supply chain / freight software as a category), candidate enumeration (a category map of supply-chain software sub-categories), segment or sub-market evaluation and scoring (two tailored screening criteria, budget source and implementation, plus a five-point checklist), market sizing (BVP's own estimates). It is written from the INVESTOR's side (which startups to back), not as a founder's step-by-step selection method.

## Strategies or frameworks described

### "Our thesis for supply chain SaaS" - two tailored evaluation criteria: Budget and Implementation
- Origin and intended context: B2B; a venture investor evaluating early-stage supply-chain SaaS startups against the same benchmarks BVP uses for "best-in-class vertical SaaS companies", with two industry-specific additions. Global/US enterprise supply chain and logistics; not agriculture-specific.
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim, author's ordering):
  - Framing: "As we evaluate new SaaS businesses in the supply chain industry, we still rely on the same benchmarks and criteria that we have typically used to assess best-in-class vertical SaaS companies over the past decade. However, we have certainly tailored much of our guidelines to the industry. In particular, we have focused on understanding where the budget will come from for a given product and what is required to implement the product, as we think both of these criteria will influence how challenging it may be to build a hyper-growth software startup in this market."
  - "Budget: Traditional wisdom in the industry said software providers needed to sell to large enterprise shippers to access any substantial software budget. While shippers still hold most of the purse strings, some companies are bucking this trend and going directly to service providers, or even carriers as their beachhead market, despite these players often having much smaller IT budgets. Either model can work, but selling to carriers or brokers requires a special product that can demonstrate value on day one, and justify its cost by offsetting operating expenses within these organizations, thus turning opex budget into IT budget."
  - "Implementation: Supply chain software implementations have traditionally looked a lot like ERP implementations, requiring long integration phases, often aided by teams of consultants to install and operate the product. We are specifically looking for products that can be implemented very quickly, such that they can demonstrate value in a matter of days and be live in weeks, not months. This aspect not only enables a much more seamless onboarding process but can also completely change the speed and efficiency of a company's go-to-market strategy. Companies that have figured out innovative ways to streamline implementation can lead their sales process with strong hooks like risk-free POCs or lightweight cost savings assessments that prove their value upfront. This is certainly easier said than done, but we believe that the next phase of supply chain SaaS companies will leverage clever integration hacks to get up and running quickly and design their interfaces to be easily used by industry veterans, not specialist consultants."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: (1) which party holds the software budget for the product (enterprise shippers vs service providers/brokers vs carriers); (2) whether the product can show value "on day one" and pay for itself out of the buyer's operating expenses ("turning opex budget into IT budget"); (3) implementation time (value in days, live in weeks); (4) whether the sales motion can open with "risk-free POCs or lightweight cost savings assessments". No quantitative thresholds are given.
- Output produced: a qualitative judgement of how hard it will be to build a "hyper-growth software startup" for a given product and buyer; implicitly, the choice of beachhead buyer (shipper vs carrier/broker).
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: stated: "Either model can work"; "This is certainly easier said than done". Reader inference: the lens is venture-scale growth, so it screens for large budgets and fast expansion rather than for the largest measurable customer saving; it says nothing about how to rank one shipper group or carrier type against another; 2021 vintage, before the 2022-23 freight downturn (reader note, not from the source: Convoy, cited approvingly here, ceased operations in 2023).
- Key quotes (section "Our thesis for supply chain SaaS"): see the two bullets above; also "most of these legacy products are really systems of record, not systems of engagement, as is evident from the fact that 50% of the largest enterprise shippers in the U.S. still rely on some combination of email and spreadsheets to actually manage supply chains day-to-day." (section "How big is this market").

### Five-point checklist of "unique qualities" BVP backs
- Origin and intended context: same as above (investor checklist for supply-chain SaaS opportunities).
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim list, section "Our thesis for supply chain SaaS"): "In general, we are looking to partner with founders and back opportunities that possess these unique qualities:
  - Digital infrastructure that connects siloed data sources and SaaS applications that streamline workflows and optimize supply chain operations
  - Software that can embrace and extend legacy systems of record with seamless implementation and onboarding
  - Products that can tap into large shipper budgets or help incumbent service providers drive net margin improvement
  - Products that offer sticky long-term value, extending beyond short term cost savings
  - Teams with substantial industry experience and empathy"
- Criteria, inputs and data required: qualitative; no scoring or weights.
- Output produced: back / do not back.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: reader inference: point 4 ("extending beyond short term cost savings") is in tension with a fee-from-savings model; the source treats cost savings as the entry hook, not the durable value.
- Key quotes: the list above (verbatim).

### "Mapping the market" - category map of supply chain software (candidate enumeration)
- Origin and intended context: BVP's tracking map of SaaS categories; "We have tried to focus largely on SaaS solutions, which is why you don't see some tech-enabled service providers on the map, or some of the more capital-intensive and asset-heavy categories, like autonomous vehicles and warehousing solutions." The graphic itself is an image and was not read; the categories below come from the prose.
- Mechanism and steps (author's ordering): the map's named categories are (1) Digital infrastructure, split into "1) increased digital interoperability and 2) transactional pipes for digital bookings" (the latter headed "Digital execution networks"); (2) Workflow automation and optimization tools; (3) Supplier intelligence; (4) Developer platforms. The authors say they are "more dialed into ... workflow automation and optimization, digital bookings platforms, and supply chain intelligence & collaboration. Plus, we also have an emerging thesis around the demand for developer tools".
  - Digital interoperability: "Despite the gains in visibility, data connectivity remains one of the top needs among enterprise shippers. In particular, we are seeing demands for better data at common bottlenecks and pain points within supply chain operations, such as procurement, first mile, drayage, customs clearance, and last mile." Data must be harnessed "whether that be integrating with legacy electronic data interchange (EDI) protocols dating back to the 1980s, scraping data from email feeds, extracting critical information from scanned documents, or simply harmonizing data across multiple APIs."
  - Digital execution networks: "Booking freight is still a highly manual process, in most cases. Considering this, we're excited about platforms that can integrate with both carriers and existing service providers, like freight forwarders and brokers, to provide the pipes for instantly bookable freight, without eliminating the value-added services that these providers offer." Example Cargo.one (air cargo, carriers <-> forwarders); "These systems require robust integrations into large networks of carriers, which can take time to build".
  - Workflow automation and optimization: "The supply chain industry is full of inefficient workflows that cause specialized workers to waste their time on low-value tasks, like data entry." Automation of "data entry and invoice processing"; decision optimization because "supply chain operators tend to make many crucial decisions based on outdated information, or on gut intuition." Examples Vector.ai (forwarder document automation) and Optimal Dynamics (trucking carriers and enterprise shippers: network planning, load-to-asset assignment, fleet utilization).
  - The wedge rule (verbatim): "We are most interested in products that leverage automation or optimization as a wedge to provide immediate ROI to customers and thus get in the door quickly. Longer-term we think that these systems will have opportunities to expand further throughout their customers' use cases and take on higher-value tasks over time."
  - Supplier intelligence: upstream supplier risk, audits, compliance; "This has unlocked larger budgets for software solutions that can provide enterprises with better visibility, risk management, and communication across their upstream suppliers."; growth via "natural network effects between suppliers and buyers".
  - Developer platforms: vertical APIs for "routing, tracking, logistics procurement, insurance, and pricing optimization"; example Zoba.
- Criteria, inputs and data required: the map is a taxonomy, not a scored ranking; the implicit sort key is "unmet needs of supply chain professionals" and the wedge rule above.
- Output produced: a list of software categories and, within them, named pain points (procurement, first mile, drayage, customs clearance, last mile; data entry; invoice processing; booking; load assignment) that can serve as a checklist of software-improvable problems.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: stated: SaaS-only, excludes asset-heavy categories and tech-enabled service providers. Reader inference: organised by software function, not by shipper industry or carrier type, so it does not enumerate two-sided sub-markets directly; it is US/enterprise oriented and 2021-dated.
- Key quotes: "limited connectivity and interoperability between data silos are still some of the most significant barriers to faster innovation in this market. The logistics industry has always needed to accommodate the lowest common denominator medium for communicating information anywhere in the world, which in many cases is still paper documentation, phone, and email." (section "Digital infrastructure").

### "Drivers of new opportunities for software in supply chains" (timing / why-now screen)
- Origin and intended context: macro argument for the category, 2021.
- Mechanism and steps: two drivers. "1. Massive shocks to the system" (pandemic e-commerce boom, Brexit, US-China trade tensions): "According to a McKinsey survey of enterprise supply chain leaders, 93% cite plans to increase resilience. And surprisingly only 11% of leaders cited budget constraints as a blocker to supply chain transformation." "2. Increased digitization": IoT/sensor data, "cloud-based systems of record like Cargowise, and data aggregators like Project44 and FourKites"; "We like to draw a loose analogy to the evolution of the fintech ecosystem, where middleware infrastructure providers like Plaid made it much easier to build modern application-layer software on top of legacy banking systems."
- Criteria, inputs and data required: external survey evidence of budget priority; presence of digital middleware to build on.
- Output produced: a "why now" argument; not a selection procedure.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques: reader inference: a readiness test (is there a system of record / data pipe to build on?) is transferable to any candidate sub-market.
- Key quotes: as above.

### Market sizing (BVP estimates)
- "Supply chain and logistics is a massive industry-a nearly $10 trillion market representing more than 10% of the global GDP today ... adding roughly ~$500 billion of market value each year." Core supply chain software (TMS/WMS, "on-premise, ERP-like systems") "represents about a $25 billion market today", with "over 10% annualized growth" expected; "we estimate that the supply chain software market can expand to north of $100 billion of SaaS revenue" over ten years. Footnote: "*All our market numbers are estimations made by Bessemer, backed by research and data." Label: source's own estimates, unverified here.

### Examples, case studies and data sources used by the author
- Portfolio and named companies: Shippo (multi-carrier shipping rates for e-commerce, BVP's first supply-chain investment, 2017), Cargo.one (air freight booking platform), Vector.ai (forwarder document automation), Optimal Dynamics (trucking decision optimization), Zoba (urban mobility fleet optimization API), Flexport, Convoy, Stord, Flexe, Project44, FourKites, Cargowise; analogies to Toast, Procore, ServiceTitan (vertical SaaS) and Plaid (fintech middleware).
- Data: a McKinsey survey of enterprise supply chain leaders (93% / 11%); the Kiel Institute for the World Economy ("global industrial output has stagnated since early 2021 due to supply chain issues"); BVP's own market estimates; the "50% of the US' largest importers still rely on spreadsheets" figure (chart; source not stated).

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
Judgement: This is the most directly usable of the three batch items for Q1 because it speaks to both sides of the project's sub-market definition: it names the shipper side as the party that "still hold[s] most of the purse strings" and the carrier/broker side as a viable beachhead only when a product can "demonstrate value on day one" and pay for itself out of operating expenses, which is exactly the "fee out of savings" constraint. Its named pain points (procurement, first mile, drayage, customs, last mile; data entry, invoice processing, manual booking, gut-feel load assignment) are a ready checklist of software-improvable, process-shaped problems to look for inside any candidate sub-market, and its "immediate ROI wedge, then expand" rule is a method-level heuristic for the segment stage. Its limits for our purpose: it does not rank shipper industries or carrier types against each other, it is organised by software function rather than by freight flow, it is 2021 and investor-lensed (hyper-growth, large budgets), and it contains nothing about agriculture, so it cannot test the origin hypothesis. Clusterable method contributions: budget-holder test; day-one-value / opex-offset test; implementation-time test; wedge-then-expand; data-infrastructure readiness as a precondition.
