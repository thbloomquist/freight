# R22 - Part I: The future of AI is vertical (Vertical AI Roadmap)
- URL: https://www.bvp.com/atlas/part-i-the-future-of-ai-is-vertical | Author: Kent Bennett, Byron Deeter, Mike Droesch, Maha Malik, Sam Bondy, Brian Feinstein, Sameer Dholakia, Caty Rea, Alex Yuditski and Aia Sarycheva, Bessemer Venture Partners (Atlas) | Year: 2024 (published Sep 3, 2024) | Type: primary (VC roadmap essay, part 1 of a four-part series) | Access: free; full HTML downloaded with curl and the whole article text read (FAQ, "Why build now", "Key opportunities", "core vs. supporting workflows", "moats"); any in-article images were not examined | Accessed: 2026-09-14
- Decision the resource addresses:
  - vertical selection: which industries and which workflows inside them suit AI-enabled vertical software;
  - market sizing: TAM expansion through labour and services spend.
  - It gives criteria and examples, not a scoring procedure. Part IV, "Bessemer's investment framework", is not in study_links.md and was not read.

## Strategies or frameworks described

### 1. Three "Key opportunities in Vertical AI" (author's headings: Expand total addressable markets; Unlock new functions and verticals; Provide unprecedented value)
- Origin and intended context: B2B. The authors write from "one of the largest vertical SaaS portfolios in venture" (Mindbody, Shopify, Procore). The essay is aimed at founders building LLM-native vertical applications in 2024. *(verified)*
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim, author's order):
  1. Expand TAMs: "Historically, software builders have focused on the largest TAMs, ignoring niche categories where it would be difficult to build a big business. Vertical AI is able to unlock markets that previously would have been perceived as too small to build a sustainable SaaS business because they are significantly increasing the scope of value delivered through AI." Sizing signal: "The US Bureau of Labor Statistics cites software spend as 1% of the US GDP, and the Business and Professional Services industry — dominated by repetitive language tasks — at 13%."
  2. Unlock new functions and verticals: "In many cases, the ROI of a software solution alone wasn't high enough to convince technophobic decision-makers and justify the upfront costs of setting up the required infrastructure, implementing new processes, and training employees."
  3. Provide unprecedented value: "The most attractive markets to build Vertical AI companies will likely be in contexts where AI facilitates or completes work that was previously impossible or too expensive to achieve with human labor alone, and so the work was not being done or being done poorly. A common, successful use case for AI is automating or streamlining workflows by reviewing significantly more data than humans would previously have been able to audit."
- Criteria, inputs and data required:
  - labour spend on repetitive language or quantitative tasks, relative to software spend;
  - whether earlier SaaS ROI was too low to justify implementation;
  - whether the work is currently not done or done poorly;
  - the volume of data a human would have to review.
- Output produced: a judgement that a vertical or function is newly addressable. There are no scores.
- Rationale and evidence base:
  - Portfolio analysis: "LLM-native companies in this cohort (with founding dates of 2019 to present) have quickly reached 80% of the average contract value (ACV) of the traditional core vertical SaaS systems, and are growing ~400% year-over-year, while still maintaining a healthy ~65% gross margin." The cohort size is not stated and the figures are unaudited.
  - Exits cited: CaseText, acquired by Thomson Reuters for $650M; Lexion, acquired by DocuSign for $165M.
  - A BLS statistic whose table is not cited.
  - A prediction: "we predict that Vertical AI's market capitalization will be at least 10x the size of legacy Vertical SaaS".
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques:
  - *Reader inference:* this is advocacy by an investor with a portfolio in the category.
  - No weights or thresholds are given.
  - "Labour spend" is a ceiling, not a demonstrated willingness to pay.
- Key quotes: see above.

### 2. Core versus supporting workflows (section "Vertical AI for core vs. supporting workflows")
- Origin and intended context: the same essay, used to decide which workflow inside a vertical to attack.
- Mechanism and steps (verbatim):
  - Definitions: "Core workflows are those that are a primary function of a job; for example, financial modeling for an investment banker or contract drafting for a lawyer. Supporting workflows are those incidental to a job or business but still necessary; for example, marketing and patient relationship management for a dentist (e.i. Weave) or freight procurement for a shipper (i.e. GoodShip)."
  - FAQ summary: "Supporting workflows: Ancillary or back-office tasks (e.g., marketing for dentists, procurement for shippers). AI adoption here often faces less resistance and higher ROI."
  - Automation propensity (core workflows): "Today, text-and quantitative-heavy work have the highest propensity for automation. That's why we tend to see more vertical AI solutions addressing the core workflows of industries that are dominated by traditional office work —- such as in legal and professional services — rather than those that require significant manual labor — such as home services and manufacturing."
  - Customer desire: "just because a core workflow has a high propensity for automation doesn't necessarily mean it's a good use case for AI. A target customer's desire to automate a given workflow matters too, and that will vary significantly across sectors." Example: bankers may automate slides but not client presentations "because of the importance of relationships in the space."
  - Why supporting workflows can be better targets: "Supporting workflows may be better targets for Vertical AI specifically because they are ancillary to the job of the target customer, and therefore are typically the type of work that can be delegated to and completed satisfactorily by AI."
  - The risk of supporting workflows: "many tech-forward horizontal incumbents in these sectors have already begun incorporating AI into their platforms, and vertical AI startups will need to deliver meaningfully better solutions in order to compete."
  - The vertical edge: "better poised to understand sector-specific nuances and integrate with underlying systems (such as CRMs)".
  - The TAM caution: "founders will need to pay close attention to the potential TAM of any solution given that these forms of defensibility may come at the expense of market size and therefore require the layer cake approach".
  - Closing rule: "founders need to have good judgment, a deep understanding of customer needs, effective feedback channels, and a clear grasp of the regulatory landscape in order to identify the specific sectors and tasks that are well-suited for an AI solution. Remember: just because something can be automated, doesn't mean it should."
- Criteria, inputs and data required:
  - core or supporting;
  - text- and quantitative-heavy versus manual labour;
  - the customer's desire to automate, and the role of relationships in the work;
  - horizontal competition in that workflow;
  - the need to stack products (the "layer cake") to reach TAM.
- Output produced: a choice of first workflow (the wedge) inside a vertical.
- Rationale and evidence base:
  - The rationale is quoted above: supporting work is delegable and meets less resistance.
  - The evidence is portfolio observation (Weave, GoodShip, Fieldguide, EvenUp, Abridge) and author authority. There is no systematic data.
- Assumptions, limitations, critiques:
  - Stated: supporting workflows face horizontal AI incumbents and may be smaller.
  - *Reader inference:* "freight procurement for a shipper" is named as a supporting workflow. That is the only freight reference, and transportation is not ranked against other sectors.
- Key quotes: see above.

### 3. "A note on Vertical AI moats"
- Mechanism: defensibility comes from "data, product depth, economic value, etc." The FAQ also lists "proprietary data, depth of product integration, and economic value delivered" and "sector-specific knowledge and integration with industry systems". The detail is deferred to Part IV.
- Rationale and evidence base: author assertion.
- Relevance: "economic value delivered" is a moat, which fits a fee-from-savings model.

## Reader's judgement on relevance to a two-sided freight sub-market choice
*Judgement:*
- R22 adds five screening dimensions any freight candidate can be scored on:
  1. automation propensity of the target work (text- and quantitative-heavy office work, not driving or handling);
  2. the customer's desire to automate it (relationship-heavy work resists; see R27 on brokerage being "relationship-driven");
  3. core versus supporting (supporting meets less resistance but more horizontal competition and a smaller TAM);
  4. work that is currently not done or done poorly;
  5. labour spend relative to software spend.
- Shippers' freight procurement is explicitly a supporting workflow in Bessemer's framing, which is a direct pointer for candidate generation.
- It gives no scoring scale, no sector ranking and no fee logic beyond "economic value delivered" and "high-ROI solutions".
- The labour-versus-software headroom argument needs sub-market labour data to become a score.
