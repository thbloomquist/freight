**Words used here:**
- **Overcharge / billed wrong:** a carrier bill higher than the agreed rate, tariff or service actually performed. **Provable:** the shipper's own documents (agreement, fuel table, bill of lading, delivery receipt) show it is wrong.
- **Identified / found / flagged:** an auditor says a charge is wrong. **Credited / collected / recovered:** the money actually came back (refund, credit memo, or a short-pay the carrier accepted).
- **Pre-payment audit:** bills checked before payment; an error is simply not paid. **Post-payment audit (look-back):** paid bills re-checked later, and claims filed.
- **Claim window:** the time a shipper has to contest a bill. **In-window spend:** dollars on bills still inside that window on the day of the audit.
- **The 180-day rule:** 49 U.S.C. 13710(a)(3)(B): a shipper must contest a motor carrier's bill within 180 days of receiving it.
- **Part 378:** the federal regulation on how motor carriers handle overcharge, duplicate-payment and overcollection claims (acknowledge in 30 days, decide in 60).
- **Tariff:** a carrier's published price and rules book. **Contract carriage / 14101(b):** a written shipper-carrier contract may "expressly waive" federal rights and remedies, which can replace the 180-day rule with the contract's own terms.
- **Broker / 3PL:** a middleman that books the carrier and bills the shipper itself. **Bill-to party:** whoever receives and pays the carrier's bill.
- **Offset (setoff):** deducting a disputed amount from a later payment to the same supplier.
- **Dilution (factoring):** the share of invoice value a seller never collects, for any reason (disputes, credits, short pays).
- **PowerTrack:** the U.S. Bank freight-payment system DoD uses. **GBL:** government bill of lading. **HHG:** household goods (moving people's belongings). **Tender:** a carrier's rate offer to the government.

### AM1.3 Reading [our call]

1. **The size of the leak depends on the stage at which it is measured.** In federal data, billing-stage checks flag about 3-5% of what carriers first bill (pooled; 0.7-9.4% by contractor or agency), while post-payment audits after those checks find 0.07%, and one mismatched DoD figure sits near 1.5%. The model's 1.5% lies between the two regimes, so neither confirms nor refutes it.
2. **The government pre-payment rates are not a private shipper's rate.** They are flagged (not credited) amounts, reported by the auditors themselves, measured against government tenders, with household goods and some passenger bills mixed in. They show that **billing against a written rate goes wrong often enough to matter at the invoice stage**; they do not say a mid-sized manufacturer's bills are 3-5% wrong.
3. **Part of every "overcharge" can be the shipper's own fault.** In the GAO rail case, 43% came from the Army's own paperwork, and the SJ repost says half of bills of lading are wrong. The kill test's definition (carrier-wrong *and* provable, excluding legitimate corrections) is the right one; vendor rates do not use it.
4. **Where a 3PL holds the carrier rates, even a neutral auditor finds nothing** (DTCI). This supports A09-14: the broker share of a shipper's freight cuts the base an audit can bite on.
5. **No figure here moves Test 1's lines.** The look-back is still the only measurement of A09-01 for our segment.

**Reading [our call]:** "catch rate" has two halves the model folds into one number: **recall** (what share of real errors the audit finds; GAO's 72-78% is an upper bound) and **precision** (what share of flags survive the client's and the carrier's review). The Texas AP case shows precision can be far below recall even for "simple" duplicates. The look-back should record both: flags, flags the shipper accepts, and flags the documents prove.

**Reading [our call]:**
1. **Nothing new measures what carriers credit a mid-sized private shipper.** The model's 75% rests where check AB left it.
2. **Leverage is contractual.** Whether a shipper can short-pay or offset depends on whose terms govern: a broker's terms can forbid it; a shipper's own carriage terms can allow it after 90 days. That is a question for each customer's paperwork.
3. **A late claim is not void, only weak.** After 180 days the carrier *may* pay; nothing makes it.
4. **The carrier-side ceiling holds.** All-cause dilution "<3%" in a factoring example sits with FedEx Freight's 1.3-2.6% of revenue in all-cause give-backs, but both are illustrations or aggregates, not overcharge-credit rates.

### AM4.4 Share of a look-back still inside the window [our calc]

Assumptions: bills spread evenly over the look-back; the clock starts on the invoice date; "day 0" means claims go in on the day the audit starts. Formula: in-window share = max(0, window − filing delay) ÷ look-back length, capped at 100% [C-AM55].

| Window | 12-month look-back, file on day 0 | 12-month, file on day 30 | 24-month, file on day 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 180 days (statute, LTL tariffs) | **49.3%** | **41.1%** | 24.7% |
| 30 days (broker terms) | 8.2% | 0% (30 − 30 = 0) | 4.1% |
| 30 business days (≈42 calendar days) | 11.5% | 3.3% | 5.8% |
| 5 days (one broker) | 1.4% | 0% | 0.7% |
| 18 months (Avient for clerical errors; Amazon-Central Freight) | 100% | 100% | 75.1% |

(The day-30 cells for 30 days, 30 business days and 5 days: (30−30)/365 = 0; (42−30)/365 = 3.3%; 5−30 < 0, so 0.)

### AM4.5 Reading [our call]

1. **The model's ~50% holds only for carrier-billed tariff freight, and only if claims go in at once.** A month's delay takes it to about 41%.
2. **Broker-billed loads are the bigger risk to a look-back product.** By their text, neither 13710 nor Part 378 governs the shipper's bill from a broker, and public SMB broker terms allow 5-30 days. On those terms a 12-month look-back has almost nothing in-window. Monthly or pre-payment checking is the only version that reaches broker-billed freight. This is a design observation, not a decision.
3. **Contract freight is where the law is unsettled.** One court (with both sides agreeing) says 13710 reaches negotiated rates; another says Chapter 137's collection rules do not apply without a tariff. A written contract can lengthen or shorten the window either way. The customer's agreement, not the statute, decides; the interview question on claims clauses is the right one.
4. **The broker's record is a federal right.** Under 49 CFR 371.3(c) a shipper may see the broker's record of a load, including the broker's compensation. That is a document the look-back can ask for.
